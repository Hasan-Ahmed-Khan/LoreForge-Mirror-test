She finished the field at the ninth hour of the morning and came in with her sleeves wet to the elbow, and stood in front of the board and said, "Thirty-four is turning."

Rusk had the rag in his hand and had been about to take the date off and put a new one up. "Thirty-four was drawn and tasted at first light."

"By you."

"By me. Same as every morning for nineteen years."

"Then you tasted it eleven hours before I did, and it has moved since, and it will move again by the time you put your date up."

Under the awning the hands had stopped working without appearing to. Ysoly came out of the second cell with the licence book under her arm and said nothing, which was her way of joining a thing.

"It is sweet. It is a hard well and always was, and a woman who has drunk butt water for six days will call anything hard—"

"Fifty-one," Sabra said.

"Fifty-one has been brack for eleven years."

"Then eleven years is long enough for a hole to change its mind. It is sweeter than four, than eleven and than thirty-four, and it is not on your board."

Rusk laughed, one note, and looked round for somebody to laugh with, and the awning did not oblige him. "It's a tongue. You've a good one. It is still a tongue, and a field can't be run off tongues."

"There is a board here," Sabra said, "because in the year sixty-six a man chalked nine numbers on it at first light and went to his breakfast."

The Warden of the post came out at that: a heavy man in his forties named Aske, Fifth Rank, in shirtsleeves, carrying the toll-book the way another man carries a hat.

"I will not have that said in my field."

"It was my field first," Sabra said.

"Then the board's sealed," said one of the Kaddals from under the awning, meaning nothing by it, and Iseul Fane turned her head. She had been at the corner of the post since before it started, out of the shade, the way she stood everywhere.

"The board is chalked. There is nothing sealed at this station and nothing stamped. Use the word you want."

That was the whole of what she contributed. It went round again — Rusk's nineteen years against Sabra's thirty-seven, one mouth against another mouth, with sixty stone lids lying out in the glare behind them saying nothing whatever.

"Master," Denet Rasca said.

He had come up from the wagons with the brass-cornered case under his arm, and he said it to Ysoly and not to any of the Wardenry, which Hanei noticed and thought about afterwards.

"I can settle it. I'm sorry — I should say I would rather not, that it takes about a minute, and that I would like the Warden to be the one who asks me. Warden."

"What is it?"

"A water-glass. Sealed of the College of Levels." He set the case on the trough coping and opened it: fleece inside, and out of the fleece a graduated tube eighteen inches long, stoppered and waxed at both ends, half full of something clear that moved slowly when he moved it, a small grey disc of lead wired at the neck. "The brine in it was drawn at the far rim under that mark. It floats against whatever you put it in. Heavier than the reference and it rides high; lighter and it goes down; and where it stops, it stops against a scale a hundred and fifteen years of arguing went into."

"It's a piece of glass," Rusk said.

"It is. The seal is what makes it evidence. Anybody can blow a tube." Denet had his sleeve back. "I want a jar with some depth, and the water drawn in front of you and not fetched to me."

Aske sent his own man down with the post's stoneware jar and Rusk went too, and the whole awning walked out after them and stood round collar thirty-four in the white while the bucket went down and came up.

The glass went into the jar. Denet held it upright with a finger on the stopper and let go.

It went down. It bobbed twice, turned a little on its own axis, and settled, and everybody bent to look at a curve of water in a tube in a stoneware pot in the middle of the largest empty place any of them would ever stand in.

"It has to stop moving," Denet said. "A minute."

Nobody spoke for the minute. It was longer than a minute of anything Hanei had ever waited through.

"Brackish. Not foul — nobody dies of it this week. A good way under the mark I would put a barrel below."

"Read the number," Rusk said, and Denet read the number.

They walked the length of the field to fifty-one and did it again, and the glass rode up.

"Sweet. Sweeter than anything I have drunk since Bell Harrow. Sweeter than four and eleven, I would guess — though I have not floated those, and I am not going to say a thing I have not floated."

Aske took the rag off Rusk's belt himself, wiped thirty-four off the board, chalked fifty-one into the gap, and stood back.

Nine numbers. The same nine as at first light, counted, and not one of them the same nine.

"How long," Sabra said, "have you had that in a box on my wagons?"

"Six days."

"And before six days?"

Denet closed the case and put the two brass catches down, one and then the other, and did not answer her; and Ysoly Tamm, who had said nothing since she came out of the cell, watched him not answer.

Then Aske went inside with the toll-book, and came out ten minutes later, and rationed the field.
