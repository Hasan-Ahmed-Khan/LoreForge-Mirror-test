"Two gallons the head, half a gallon the hask. Eleven and forty. Forty-two the day." Sabra Onn said it to nobody in the grey light, the way she had said it every dawn for thirty-one crossings, so that afterwards no one could claim it had not been said. "Ten days to water. Four hundred and twenty. And I am standing on four hundred and twenty-five."

Nobody answered her. Nobody ever did.

"That is not a grace," she said. "That is a dare."

Then she went down the string and unstopped the first butt and poured it out on the ground.

It was hard brack out of a rock cistern, ten days old, bought at a block the two hundredweight in a dry season on the only rock between the rims, and it came out of the hide in a rope as thick as a wrist and hit the brown ground and stood in a sheet and then began to go down.

Nobody said one word to her. Vesh took his hat off and put it back on. Hanei, who had been taught in twenty days that spilled water was close to a sin and had been made to say so back word for word, went and unstopped the second butt herself.

Four thousand two hundred and fifty pounds of water went into the ground at Benchmark One before the third hour of the morning, and the ground took all of it, and by the time the last butt was up-ended there was no mark left anywhere the first had gone.

Then they filled.

It took from the third hour to dusk and it took all of them. Two hide buckets on a doubled line, a chain of people from the rim to the wagons, and the butts coming down off the slings four to a wagon and going back up full. The brown ground round the hole went to a trodden slick and then to a mire and then to something a boot came out of with a sound, and by noon there were eleven people working in wet clay to the knee in the middle of the driest place any of them would ever stand in.

They drew it to the clay in a little under two hours. There was a moment when the bucket came up with a pint in it and grit, and Sabra Onn stood at the rim of a hole she had emptied and looked at the bottom of it, and nobody breathed.

Then it came back.

It came back the way it had come in the first place, from the bottom and the four sides at once, the walls going dark from the foot upward, and by the middle of the afternoon there were two feet standing in it again and they went back to work.

Denet Rasca sat on the spoil ring with a boring log across his knee and did nothing at all except watch the water and mark the leaf.

"Four times," he said, at the end of it. "Drawn to the clay four times and recovered four times, and the fourth was no slower than the first. Two hundred and seventy gallons a draw, and a little over two hours to make it again, and I am rounding down every figure in that sentence because it is the first time anybody has had them and I would rather be under."

"Say what it means," Ysoly said.

"It means a four-foot hole at the Centre of the Meniscus will yield a thousand gallons a day to two buckets and a rope and it will do it tomorrow as well." He set the pencil down. "I'm sorry. I should say the rest, because it is the part I have not been able to write for five months. A boring gives you a depth and a quality. It cannot give you a volume, and the packet does not claim one, and any advocate at either rim would have taken the packet apart on exactly that in an afternoon. Reachable is not plentiful." He looked up. "Eleven people with two spades and one night have just measured the thing the survey could not. That is the number, master. It has been made in front of everybody standing on this ground and it has not been written anywhere."

Ysoly Tamm stood in wet clay to the knee at the centre of the plain and thought about that for a while.

Then she took the tally-stick out from under her coat.

"Corrow. Get everybody. Nobody goes to the pound until this is cut."

They came, in the order people come to a thing they have been sent for in daylight, and stood round in a ring in the mire with their hands hanging, and Ysoly turned a yard of black rim-oak past heads, past ballast, past the debt face, to the face cut for water drawn.

"One thousand and eighty gallons out of the ground at Benchmark One, on the twenty-third day of a summer crossing, drawn four times to the clay and recovered four times, at two hundred and seventy the draw." She cut it while she said it, and she said it twice, and the second time she said it slowly enough for a nineteen-year-old to get it word for word. "Cut by the master of a licensed crossing in the presence of her own hands. Say it back."

Hanei said it back. All of it.

"Now the surveyor will say why it is worth anything," Ysoly said, "because I have carried this stick for thirty years and I did not know until last night."

"The fifth head of the Compact of Weights," Denet said. "'A tally cut by the master of a licensed crossing, in the presence of her own hands, is a book of account at any weighbridge within the bounds -- as good as a ledger and admissible at either rim.'" He had his hands behind his back like a man being examined. "It is not a private note. It is an instrument. It is the same law that lets a weighbridge hold her to two gallons the head against a scale, and it works in both directions, and it has done since the year forty-seven."

"And the Article cannot reach it," Sabra said.

"'Papers are the goods of him that holds them, and go off the ground in his hand.'" Denet said it flatly. "The seal lies on ground and on water and on nothing else. That stick is neither. It goes south in her coat and it is evidence at Tallow Reach."

Sabra Onn wiped her hands down her thighs and looked at thirty-six butts standing full on nine wagons, one thousand and eighty gallons, more water than the Tamm Line had ever rolled out of anywhere in thirty years, and none of it bought, and none of it carried, and none of it weighed against anybody's cylinder.

"Twenty-five days," she said, to nobody. "Eleven and forty at forty-two the day. We can stand on this ground until the middle of next month."

"Say it properly," Ysoly said.

"Nobody has to leave here for anything." Sabra's voice did not change at any point. "Not for water. In thirty-seven years I have never once said that sentence and I did not expect to live to."

The dust came up on the northern beacons at the seventh hour, and it was not one rider's dust, and it stood in the air the way only a great many wheels can make it stand.

But the rider came in first, ahead of it, alone, on four hask with no wagon, at fifty-eight, with his face the colour of a fired tile and peeling along the cheekbones and his book in his coat. He came down off the animal at the cairn like a man getting off a roof and stood holding the saddle for a moment before he trusted his legs.

"Master Tamm," Oreth Vask said. "There are two hundred people nine miles behind me and they will be on this ground at first light, and I have ridden ten days twice this season, and I am too old for the second one." He looked past her at the hole, and at the stack of a hundred blocks standing white on the brown, and at thirty-six full butts on nine wagons.

Then he took out his book and opened it, and did not write anything in it at all.
