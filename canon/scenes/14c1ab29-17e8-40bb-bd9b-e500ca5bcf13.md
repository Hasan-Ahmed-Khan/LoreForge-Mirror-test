They were nine feet down by the third hour of the morning and Hanei was in the hole.

It had gone easily to four — the brown came off the spade in slabs and the salt under it broke to the blade and went up in a bucket faster than two people could carry it away — and then the grey clay started and the whole thing slowed to inches and shoulders. They cut it four feet square, because Denet said a round hole in soft ground closes on you and a square one tells you when it is going. Lyddan dug and Hanei filled and Bris hauled, then Bris dug and Hanei hauled, then Hanei dug.

She had spent a whole night on the rope at Sixty Wells, paying hemp over dressed stone and not once allowed the bucket. She had the bucket now. It was not better.

At a little after nine feet the clay changed under the blade. It went from grey to the colour of a bruise, and heavier, and it came off the spade in a way that was almost soft, and then the hole began to take her boots.

"Out," said Ysoly, from the rim.

"It's coming—"

"Out. Now, and say it back."

"Out now." Hanei went up the ladder of cut steps with the water over the welts of her boots and stood on the brown ground with her heart going.

It came in from the bottom and from the four sides at once. Not a spring and not a rush; a seeping, everywhere, the walls going dark from the foot upward, and a flat sheet forming across the bottom of a hole that eleven people had spent seven hours digging with two spades.

They stood round it with a lamp and watched it fill for a while, because there was nothing to do about it and nowhere else to be.

By the time the lamp had burned down to the last of its oil there were two feet of water standing in the ground at the centre of the Meniscus, and it had a lamp-flame on it, moving.

Denet floated the glass on it there in the hole, on a line, and read it, and said the number aloud without being asked, and put the glass back in the fleece, and then stood holding the case because there was still nowhere on the whole plain to set it down.

Sabra Onn came out at the turn of the last shift with a tin cup in her hand.

She had been at the pound all night and her sleeves were wet to the elbow and she did not look at any of them. She knelt at the rim, and dipped the cup, and drank it off, and knelt there.

Thirty-seven years of weighing it. Sixty stone lids and a taster's board and a woman on her knees in the white with a lantern, spitting into the dark. Four hundred people gone south past a rock with what they had, and the days after the Ferry that she did not describe.

Day twenty. She had walked over the twentieth day thirty-one times.

She dipped the cup again, full, and held it out at arm's length over the ground, and turned it over, and poured the whole of it away.

It went into the brown ground and the ground took it and there was a dark patch about the size of a hand, and then the dark patch was smaller, and then it was gone.

Hanei, who had been taught in twenty days that spilled water was close to a sin and had been made to say so back word for word, stood four feet away and said nothing at all.

"Say nothing about that," Sabra said.

"No."

"Not to her and not to anybody."

"Not to her and not to anybody."

Sabra Onn got up off her knees, which took her a moment at sixty-one, and went down to the pound with the lantern held low, and walked the line of the string in the last of the dark the way she had walked it every morning of thirty-one crossings, listening.

One. Then three or four. Then the whole pound at once, forty-one animals coughing a night's salt out of their nostrils in a noise like a wall coming down slowly.

She got to the end and stood there. Then she came back up the line, slower, and stopped at the fourth team, and put her hand flat on a grey-dun shoulder gone chalk-white at the flank, and stood with it a while.

Forty.

She came out into the grey light where the wagons were and where eleven people were roping up round a hole with two feet of water standing in it, and she said it to nobody, to the ground, the way she said it every dawn.

"Two gallons the head, half a gallon the hask. Eleven and forty-one. Forty-two and a half the day." She had the tin cup still in her hand. "Ten days to water."

"Onn," Hanei said. "It's forty. The near-side on four didn't husk."

"It is forty-one until it is down."

"And there's water. There's water in the ground a hundred paces off, I dug it, I'm standing in the boots—"

"Ten days to water," Sabra Onn said, "and the butts stay full, and I will hear the figure back."

Hanei said it back. All of it, word for word, in the voice she had never got right and had stopped trying to get right, standing on brown ground in the first of the light with wet clay to the knee.

Behind them, at the cairn, Iseul Fane finished picketing her animal and walked out along the stake line past nine numbered stakes and their spoil rings, and past a stack of a hundred blocks of second-grade salt standing white on the brown, and came to the edge of a square hole four feet across, and stopped, and looked down into it for a long time at water standing within the bounds of the Meniscus.
