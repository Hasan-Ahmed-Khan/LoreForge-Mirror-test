She had a lamp on the cairn and the waxed sleeve open on her knee, and she was reading it by a light that had to be shielded from nothing, because there was no wind at the centre of the plain at night and never had been.

Ysoly came up out of the dark and stood on the other side of the stone.

"Sit down, master. There is one flat stone and you are fifty-four."

"I'll stand."

"Then we will both stand and neither of us will be honest about it," Iseul said, "and I have not the time." She waited until Ysoly sat down on the spoil and then sat down herself on the other side, with the lamp between them, and turned the sleeve round so that the writing faced the light and not her.

"I am going to read you two sentences. They are a hundred and sixty-six years apart and I do not believe anybody has ever had to read them in the same evening."

"Read them."

"Second Article. Original to the year one. 'A Warden of the Third Rank or above may revoke a licence upon the road, by breaking the tile in the holder's hand and in his sight, speaking the cause before he breaks it and before one witness that can be named; and the halves shall be kept and carried back to the roll at Bell Harrow, and the cause entered there beside the roll number.'" She did not look up. "'A Warden below the Third Rank may take up a tile and report it, and may not break it.'"

"I have heard the first half of that in a licence office."

"You have heard the part they tell an apprentice." Iseul turned the leaf. "Fourth Article, sealed in the year one hundred and sixty-seven, the last proviso. 'Provided lastly, that where the master of a lawful crossing stands upon sealed ground, holding his tile whole, he may require of the Warden, before the same two witnesses, that the survey be begun before the turn of the season next following; and the Warden shall enter the requirement upon the roll beside the seal, and the Wardenry shall appoint the surveyor and bear the cost of him.'"

She stopped there, and then read the rest of it anyway, because she was a woman who did not leave the second half of a sentence in her mouth.

"'A requirement so made is not undone by the after-breaking of the tile. But a tile broken before the speaking of it is no licence, and he that holds no licence may require nothing.'"

The crust ticked somewhere out beyond the stakes as it let go of the day.

"Say it plainly," Ysoly said.

"If your tile is whole when you speak, the requirement stands, and it stands even if I break the clay four seconds afterwards, because the men who wrote the proviso could see that far and put it in the text. If I speak the cause and break the tile first, then there is nobody standing on that ground with a licence, and there is nothing to require, and the ground is held the way Ferrant's ground has been held for fourteen years." Iseul folded the sleeve shut with her thumb inside it. "Two sentences. Whichever is spoken first governs. And I am the only mouth between the rims that can speak either of them, which I did not arrange and cannot help."

Ysoly Tamm sat with her hands on her knees and did not move for some while.

"Then why in God's name are you telling me?"

"Because if I do not tell you, then what happens on this ground in two days is not law. It is a woman on a horse arriving early." Iseul said it without heat, the way she said everything. "I have been twelve years at the floor of this Article. I have never once had to know what I would do, and I have never asked for a seat at Bell Harrow, and I have thought about that more in three days than in the twelve years."

"You could ride on. You have found eleven stakes and a bucket of wet clay."

"I have found water standing within the bounds of the Meniscus with a hundred blocks of Assembly ballast stacked beside it, and eleven people camped on it, and thirty-six wagons two days behind." Iseul turned her head. "Now hear the other side of it said properly, because you will get it badly from somebody else. In the year one hundred and sixty-six a man chalked nine numbers on a board at first light and went to his breakfast, and four hundred people went south out of that field on what he had chalked, and something over two hundred of them never reached the Ferry, and the ones who did came past this cairn on the twentieth day with brine in the butts. Your water-master filled a barrel off that board. The Fourth Article is the answer the rim gave to it, and the answer was: nobody drinks unsurveyed water within the bounds ever again. If that hole goes brack in a season and four hundred people have arranged a crossing round it, you will get the wells again with no wells to ration."

"Ferrant," Ysoly said.

"Ferrant."

"Ninety feet, forty miles out, assayed on his own spoil-heap in one morning by two Wardenry men with a Wardenry glass, capped with plain-salt and mortar inside the week. Fourteen years and no surveyor appointed. He asked in the year one hundred and ninety and again in the year one hundred and ninety-three, and he died asking."

"Yes," Iseul said. "That is why the fourth proviso is in the Article. It is the only limit in it with teeth, and it was put there by men who had watched what a Wardenry does with an indefinite hold." She looked at the lamp. "And it rides on a fired tile the size of a palm, and I am the person who can break the tile. I have been on this ground since the third hour of yesterday morning trying to find the reading in which those two facts are not both true, and there is not one."

Out past the stakes something moved in the pound and settled, and a single hask coughed and did not go on coughing.

"There is one more thing and I would rather say it tonight than in front of two hundred people." Iseul put the sleeve back inside her coat. "The Article says the Wardenry shall pay the finder the worth of the ground at the last assessed price of salt. It has never once been invoked where the finder was a Warden. Whoever I name, I have to name aloud, and the naming goes on the roll at Bell Harrow beside the seal."

She got up, and put her hand on the bronze plug in the top of the cairn without appearing to know she had done it, the way two centuries of people had.

"So somebody is going to have to tell me who found this water," she said. "And I have thought about it for a day and a night, and I do not like either answer."
