The room was hot and low and smelled of the rock: wet stone under a hide roof, lamp-black, and old paper that had never quite dried out in a hundred and fifty years. One table, one chair, a shelf of ledgers with the damp in their spines. And chained through a ring by the door, the way it hung in every counting-house and licence office on the Road, a plain iron cylinder the length of a forearm.

Vask saw Ysoly look at it.

"They sealed the Compact on this rock. Downstairs, in the year forty-seven — two councils and eleven salt houses, out here because it was the only ground between the rims that belonged to neither of them, and they sat down and decided what a pound was." He set the lamp where it would not be in anybody's eyes. "Everything I do rests on a sentence a man wrote on this rock, and the sentence is that a gallon of sweet water weighs ten pounds. Sit down, madam. There is one chair and you will have it."

"I'll stand."

"You will not, because you have been on a box seven days and I have been on a saddle ten, and one of us has to be honest about it first."

She sat. He leaned on the sill instead. Denet stood by the door with the case under his arm and did not put it down.

"I have known for a month," Vask said.

Neither of them said anything.

"I would rather you had the shape of it from me at a table than assembled it at a fire, because what you build at a fire will be uglier than the truth and harder to put down afterwards. Bram writes to me. Thecla Bram, of the hide market. Nineteen years, and I have never once paid her for a letter, which is precisely why they are worth opening." He turned a page without looking at it. "In the spring she wrote that a Warden-surveyor had come in off the centre re-levelling and gone to the licence office window to ask what a passage costs on a small line, and which masters were on their last crossing. That is all it was. Nine words at a public window."

"And you priced it," Ysoly said.

"I read it. Priced is what she does." Vask closed the book on his thumb. "There is one reason a College-sealed man does not take the Wardenry's own relay south for nothing and buys nine wagons instead, and it is that he does not want the Wardenry to know where he is going. I have been thirty-four years in this trade. I did not need a second letter."

"Then you knew before my tile was fired."

"I knew before your tile was fired, and I did not have the courtesy to say so on the quay, and you may hold that against me for the rest of your life." He looked at Denet then, properly, for the first time. "Surveyor. How deep."

Denet did not answer at once.

"You have it in a bag on nine wagons going to Tallow Reach, and there is no roll entry anywhere in the world with your name against it, and you know that better than I do." Vask waited. "There is nothing left in this room to protect. How deep."

"Between nine and fourteen feet. Eleven bores. Nine wet. Every wet one fresh."

Vask had the pen moving before the word nine was out of him — nineteen years of it, the mark going down while the sentence was still being made. Ysoly had watched him do that to her on a quay in the smoke and had never once seen him stop.

He stopped.

The pen stayed where it was on the page, and Oreth Vask looked at the wall for about four breaths, and downstairs the northbound crossing laughed at something.

"Fourteen feet," he said.

"Nine to fourteen. I would not say fourteen alone. It reads better than it is."

"It does not read better than it is. It reads exactly as it is." He put the book on the table and turned it round so it faced them, and it was already written: a page of numbered terms in a small institutional hand, dated at Bell Harrow, drafted before a bell had rung nine times over anybody.

"One. The forty-one leaves, and every note, level and log of them, bought of Denet Rasca as the finder, for four hundred blocks of first grade at Bell Harrow, on demand.

"Two. The Tamm Line's water at this station, and the twenty-two blocks at Sixty Wells, struck.

"Three. The paper on the Tamm Line's wagons taken up by the Assembly and cancelled, and the sum owing to the master on retirement paid in the autumn instead of owed.

"Four. A season's wage to each of the eleven, on the roll at Bell Harrow, for the inconvenience of a passenger.

"Five. Corrow's paper at Bram's — sixty-four blocks and the interest at a quarter — taken up and cancelled."

Ysoly Tamm read the fifth term twice and said nothing whatever about it.

"Four hundred blocks," Denet said.

"Ferrant was paid a hundred and six. The worth of forty miles of crust, at the last assessed price of salt." Vask sat down on the sill. "The last assessed price of salt is assessed by us. I say that out loud because you both know it, and because a man who pretended otherwise at this table would deserve everything that came to him afterwards. The Article says the Wardenry shall pay the finder. It does not say who holds the ruler."

"And if he does not sell."

"Then in a season, or in two years, a Warden of the Third Rank stands on that ground and speaks eleven lines and sets a lead disc on a pin, and the Wardenry pays him what we tell it to pay him, and caps the hole with plain-salt and mortar, and appoints a surveyor when it gets round to it. Ferrant asked twice and died asking." He said it without any weight on it at all. "I am the only person in this transaction offering anybody money."

"What will you do with it?"

"Nothing. I want that plain, because you will imagine I have a use for it and I have not. It goes into a box at Bell Harrow, and the box outlives me, and one day a factor who is not yet born opens it and does not know what it was for."

"That is burying it," Denet said.

"Yes."

Denet came off the door frame at last, took the pen out of the inkstand without asking, and worked a figure down the margin of the deed — the way he had worked Ysoly's water figure down the margin of her own way-bill on a tailgate in the dark, because he could not be in a room with a number and a pen and leave them apart.

"Eight thousand nine hundred and sixty gallons," he said. "Four hundred blocks, at the board downstairs. That is what you are offering, said in water." He set the pen down. "I'm sorry. I did that without thinking."

"You did it without thinking," Vask agreed, "and it is the truest thing said in this room tonight."

"I would like the night."

"You have the night. The northbound rolls at first light and anything going to Bell Harrow goes with it, and after that there is nothing north for eleven days." He opened the book again. "That is not pressure, surveyor. That is a crossing hitching up."
