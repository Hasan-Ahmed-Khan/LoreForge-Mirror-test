They came under the awning in the order people come to a thing they have been sent for at night: the two Kaddals first because they had been awake, then Vesh and Ottery, then Rooe and Sennet, then Lyddan doing up his coat, then Bris, then Sabra Onn off the trough with her sleeves wet to the elbow. Hanei stood at the guy-rope end. Pell Corrow came last and sat where he always sat, on the edge of things with something in his hands, and this time there was nothing in his hands and he had to put them on his knees.

Denet Rasca stood outside the awning line, which nobody had asked him to do.

Ysoly set the lamp on the stone and turned it down until it was doing nothing but showing where everybody's face was.

"There is an offer. I am going to read all of it, because if I read some of it you will spend seventeen days deciding what was in the part I did not read."

She read the five terms off the back of the way-bill in the flat voice she kept for figures. Four hundred blocks of first grade for the leaves. The twenty-two at the wells struck, and the water here struck. The paper on the wagons taken up and cancelled, and the master's retirement sum paid instead of owed. A season's wage to each of the eleven, on the roll at Bell Harrow.

"Five. Corrow's paper at Bram's. Sixty-four blocks and the interest at a quarter, taken up and cancelled."

She did not look at Pell when she read it. Everybody else under the awning did.

"The offer is for the packet, and the packet is not mine and it is not yours. It belongs to the man standing outside this awning, and no count taken here moves it an inch. What is mine is nine wagons and a way-bill. So the question I am putting is whether the Tamm Line carries a passenger past this rock, or whether I set him down at a licensed waystation with a Warden relay and a depot and a crossing coming through in four days, and roll south with nothing on my bill but salt." She looked up. "Say it out loud, one at a time, in front of the rest, and then it is said, and nobody gets to have meant something else at the Cold Well. Vesh."

"Set him down. I have driven for three masters and two of them lost their wagons, and I would like once in my life to be paid at a rim by somebody still trading." Vesh had his hat in his hands. "It is not more complicated than that and I am not going to make it sound better."

"Ottery."

"Set him down."

"Rooe."

"Set him down, and say the wage part again in front of everybody, because that is what I am voting for and I would rather own it."

"A season's wage to each of the eleven, for the inconvenience of a passenger," Ysoly said. "Kaddal."

"Set him down."

"The other Kaddal."

"What he said."

"Say your own."

The younger Kaddal looked at the lamp a while. "Then I don't know."

"That is your own. Bris."

"Set him down."

"Lyddan."

"Carry him." Lyddan was twenty-two and in his second season and his voice did the thing voices do. "Nothing after it. Carry him."

"Sennet."

Sennet kept his arms folded. "I would want to know what happens at the Centre. That is not an answer and I know it isn't."

"Apprentice."

"Carry him."

"Thirteen days on this plain, and it costs you nothing."

"I know. That is why I said it fast."

"Onn."

"In a moment," Sabra said. "Let Corrow go first. He has been sitting there four minutes wanting to, and it will poison the rest if it is not said."

Pell Corrow put his hands flat on his knees.

"Take the money. And I will say the rest before somebody else does, because I would rather say it. Bram has had news off me for nine years. She had a line in the spring about a surveyor asking at the licence office window what a passage costs on a small line, and she wrote to Vask, and that is how a man in Bell Harrow knew about a bag none of us had opened. There is a strip of paper on that fire over there going north at first light, and it is mine, and I wrote it two hours ago."

Nobody moved.

"I have not stolen anything from anybody here. That is not me asking you to agree; it is the only sentence I have got, and I have turned it over about four hundred times and it has not broken yet. Sixty-four blocks. Nine years. Enna is eleven, and the houses put a child on the paper at fourteen." He looked up. "Take the money. He gets four hundred blocks, we get a season each, my paper goes, and the master keeps her wagons and goes home to a roof — which is what every one of you has wanted for her since Bell Harrow and none of you will say in front of her."

"That is a good argument," Ysoly said.

"It's the true one."

"It is both. That is what makes it a good one." She did not raise her voice at all. "Onn."

Sabra Onn stood with her wet sleeves and the cup still in her hand.

"Two gallons the head," she said. "Half a gallon the hask. Eleven and forty-one."

Pell shut his eyes.

"He is still eleven," Sabra said, to the awning generally. "He drinks his two gallons and waters his string and ropes his wagon at dawn like the rest of us, and anybody with a different idea about that can take it up with me at the trough. I have seventeen days of brack ahead of me and forty-one head to get to the Cold Well, and I am sixty-one years old."
