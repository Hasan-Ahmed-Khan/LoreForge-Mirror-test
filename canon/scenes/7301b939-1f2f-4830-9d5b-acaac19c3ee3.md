The husking started in the dark, and Sabra Onn walked the pound with the lantern low and let it get to the end before she said anything, and it got to the end, and she came back.

"Forty-one," she said, to nobody.

Pell Corrow had a harness needle in his teeth and the packet across his knee on the tailgate of the fourth wagon. He had waxed his own thread the night before, sitting up, and he did the head of the bag in a running stitch that went nowhere near the old holes, and it did not match, and the new thread was pale against hide that had been carried against a man's body for five months.

"You could go through the same holes," Hanei said.

"You could." Pell did not look up. "A bag mended along its own stitching is a bag somebody wanted put back the way it was. That's worth opening. This is a bag that got sat on." He bit the thread and looked at his own work with no pleasure at all. "Nobody steals a mended thing, girl. That is the entire trade I have."

Hanei put it in the locker under the seat and her blanket on top of it, and it weighed a pound and thirteen ounces, and it was heavier going in than it had been coming out, and she knew perfectly well that that was not true and could not make it stop being true.

Denet Rasca had the two lead discs in his coat pocket. He had picked them up off the bench in the end, after standing over them some time, having failed to work out what a man does with two seals that have been struck once and cannot be struck again.

Out in the field, twenty-two blocks lighter, the wagons rode a hand higher on their axles than they had riding in, and every drover on the line had seen it and none of them had said so to the master.

Ysoly came down the string at the last of the dark with the licence book under her arm.

"Apprentice. You were owed this yesterday and did not get it, and that was mine and not yours." She did not stop walking, so Hanei walked. "You are not going back. You have the pound from here. Forty-one head, the half-gallon, the shifts, the count off the trough. You call it to Sabra Onn at dusk, she calls it to me, and I cut it. If you are ever wrong you come and tell me you were wrong before somebody else tells me you were right."

Hanei repeated it back word for word, got to the end, found she could not make her voice do the last part, and did it again anyway.

"Onn is behind you. Say it to her."

Sabra Onn had watered forty-one head across thirty-one crossings and had never lost a traveller to thirst in any of them, and she stood in the grey light with her arms folded and let a nineteen-year-old say her own numbers back to her. When it was done she said, "Fifty-one. Not four and not eleven. Fifty-one is the only hole in this field that anybody proved," and went up to the head of the string.

Then she said it, the way she said it every dawn, to nobody, to the yard, so that afterwards no one could claim it had not been said.

"Two gallons the head, half a gallon the hask. Eleven and forty-one. Forty-two and a half the day. Seven days to water."

Nobody answered her. Nobody ever did.

By the post wall Rusk was putting up the day's date under the last column in his round careful hand. Nine numbers. Four, eleven, nineteen, twenty-six, forty, forty-three, fifty, fifty-one, fifty-eight.

The station's own people came down with their cans while he was still writing, and went to four, and to eleven, and to nineteen. Two men stood at the head of the row a while looking at fifty-one, the way you look at a stranger who has moved into a house you have walked past all your life, and then went to twenty-six.

Iseul Fane rode out at first light the way she had ridden out ahead of trains for twelve years: north to the beacon, then south along it, the crossing stamp on a thong at her neck and the statute in a waxed sleeve she had not opened in four years and had not needed to open yesterday either. She lifted a hand to Ysoly going past. Ysoly lifted one back.

Fourteen days to the centre. Seven to the Ferry and seven on from it, a Warden of the Third Rank riding half a mile out in front the whole way, and forty-one leaves of paper in a locker under a girl's blanket, stitched shut with a drover's thread.

Ysoly Tamm watched her get small against the glare and then stop being anything at all. Then she turned round and counted the water butts, touching each at the hoop, all the way down to the ninth wagon.

Fifteen wet. Seventeen gallons in the sixteenth. Twenty dry and stopped.

Then she went back to the first wagon and counted them again.
