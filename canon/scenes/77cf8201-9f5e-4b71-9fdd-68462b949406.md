She had the sentence.

Iseul Fane had read it to her at the cairn two nights before, twice, and Ysoly Tamm had carried it since the way she carried a figure — under the mask against the wheel noise, turning it until the words went smooth.

I require of the Warden, before these two witnesses, that the survey be begun before the turn of the season next following.

Before these two witnesses. The Warden had stopped on that and said it again, because the proviso wanted the same two who had been named for the seal and not any two people standing about in a mire.

She looked to her right. Oreth Vask, with the book shut on his thumb and his face the colour of a fired tile.

She looked to her left. Sabra Onn, sleeves wet to the elbow, the tin cup still in her hand.

Two.

Then Ysoly Tamm did the thing she had done at the tailgate of the ninth wagon every dusk for thirty years, and had done at a weighbridge in the kiln smoke on the morning this crossing began, and would have told anyone who asked was not a habit but the trade itself.

She looked again. Vask. Onn.

Two.

The clay let go between her hand and the Warden's.

It was not loud. Fired clay in two fists does not crack; it gives once, dry, like a knuckle, and then there was a weight in her palm that was two weights and neither of them the right shape. The break had gone across the roll number, so that half the Wardenry's stamp lay under the glaze in her hand and half of it in Iseul Fane's, and the broken faces were white and coarse and had never been glazed at all.

Nine miles north the fires of thirty-six wagons stood up in a line and knew nothing whatever about it.

Ysoly said it anyway.

She said the whole of it, correctly, in the flat voice she kept for figures, before Oreth Vask and before Sabra Onn, standing in wet clay to the knee with two pieces of a licence in her fist: that she required of the Warden that the survey at Benchmark One be begun before the turn of the season next following.

Iseul Fane let her finish. That was the one thing she did.

"'A requirement so made is not undone by the after-breaking of the tile,'" Iseul said. "'But a tile broken before the speaking of it is no licence, and he that holds no licence may require nothing.'"

"I know what it says. You read it to me."

"I did, and I am going to say the rest out loud here rather than let you hear it in four years off a man paid to say it. There is nobody standing here holding a licence. There is no requirement, and no season, and no clock. The ground is held until the hazard is surveyed and lifted, and the Wardenry appoints the surveyor."

"Would you have entered it?"

"Yes."

Ysoly turned her hand over and looked at the halves lying in it.

"Did you hurry it?"

The crust ticked out past the stakes, letting go of the day, and one hask coughed in the pound and did not go on coughing.

"I have been asking myself that since my hand closed," Iseul said. "I will be asking it in ten years. So: I do not know. The Second Article says the cause is spoken and then the tile is broken, and it gives no interval. I do not know what my hand was doing."

Denet Rasca had come up from the second stake. He got as far as "Is there any—" and stopped, because there was not; and then, being the man he was, took the pencil out of his coat, and stood holding it over nothing at all, and put it back.

"There is nothing," he said. "I have been looking since she started speaking. Not in the statute and not in the Compact."

Oreth Vask opened his book and wrote it all, without hurry, the way he had done it to her on a quay for nineteen years: the seal, the witnesses, the finder, the cause, the break, the requirement spoken after. The last thing he put down was the hour.

"Master Tamm. I am sorry that it went in the order that—"

He did not finish it. He never finished them.

Sabra Onn, named on the Wardenry's roll that evening twice over — once as witness to the sealing of the water and once as witness to the breaking of the tile — said nothing whatever, and went on saying nothing for eleven days.

Iseul Fane put her hand out, palm up, into the space between them a second time.

"The halves are kept and carried back to the roll at Bell Harrow, and the cause entered beside your roll number. I do not take them off you. Give them to me."

Ysoly gave her the two pieces.

The Warden took out the cloth the lead disc had come out of an hour before — wrapped round it in a saddlebag since the year one hundred and ninety-one, and empty now — and folded the halves into it, and put it away.

"I will enter that you spoke the requirement, and the hour of it, and that the tile was broken before you spoke," she said. "That is not the requirement. It will not run a clock and it will not appoint a surveyor, and no clerk at Bell Harrow will read it twice. I am telling you what it is worth so that you do not go south believing it is worth something else."

Hanei stood at the guy-rope end with the clay dried grey to her elbows and understood, the way a person understands a thing years before they can say it, that she had watched the whole of it and had not seen anything happen. A woman had looked at two people, and looked at them again. That was the whole of what there had been to see.

Ysoly Tamm went down the string with her hand out, touching every butt at the hoop as she came to it.

Thirty-six wet. Nothing dry and stopped anywhere on nine wagons: one thousand and eighty gallons out of the ground at the centre of the Meniscus.

She got to the ninth wagon, turned round, came back up the line and counted them again.

There was nothing wrong with the count. There never had been.
