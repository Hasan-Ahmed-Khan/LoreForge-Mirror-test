She went down at the ninth hour of the morning, four hundred paces short of the pound, walking a slow curve out of the line of the string with her head low and her eyes closed against nothing.

"Cut her," Pell Corrow said, and had the knife out of his boot before he had finished saying it, because a downed animal still in harness takes the wagon over and the wagon takes the string, and everybody on the Meniscus learns that before they learn the ration.

She was the near-side leader on four, and she was nineteen years old, and Ysoly Tamm had crossed the plain on her twenty-two times.

They got the traces off her and the yoke-chain clear and stood back, and she lay on the brown ground with her flank going chalk-white where the salt of three weeks had dried on it, and coughed once, which was worse than not coughing.

"Onn," Ysoly said.

"There is nothing to be done for the staggers and there never was." Sabra was already on her knees at the animal's head, with her hand flat on the shoulder where a drover puts it. "She had a half-gallon every day of this crossing and she drank at every lid I cut, and she is nineteen. Do not go looking for a fault in the water. There isn't one."

It took the better part of an hour. Nobody talked through it and Pell talked less than anybody.

Then Bris said, "Where do we leave her?"

On the crust you leave them. There is no other answer and there never has been: the plain is grey-white salt over grey clay to the depth of a man's arm, and a spade turns on it, and every drover between the rims has walked away from an animal lying out under the glare with the ridges going away from her in plates. It is the thing about the trade that nobody at a rim ever asks about twice.

Hanei looked at the ground.

"We can dig," she said.

They dug. It took five of them the rest of the morning and the whole of the afternoon and they broke the handle of the second spade doing it, and the brown came off in slabs and the salt under it broke to the blade and the grey clay below that was the worst work any of them had ever done, and it was the first grave any hask had ever had on the Meniscus in two hundred years of the Road.

The argument ran across it while they worked, because there was nowhere on nine wagons to have it and there never had been.

"She speaks tomorrow," Vesh said, from the bottom, handing a spadeful up. "Then it is sealed and we are on the road the day after and we are at the Cold Well in ten days and at the far rim in twenty-one, and we get paid, and I have driven for three masters and two of them lost their wagons and I have never once been paid at a rim by a woman still trading."

"You have been paid every crossing you ever made with this Line," Sabra said.

"I have. That is why I am saying it out loud instead of behind her."

"And the hole?" Hanei said.

"Capped. Plain-salt and mortar, the way they did Ferrant. I am sorry, girl. You dug it and I helped you and it is the best thing I have ever been part of and it is going under a mortar cap on Thursday."

Rooe said, "Say the rest of it," and Vesh said, "There is no rest of it. I am forty-six," and went back to digging.

"I am thirty-four," Pell Corrow said.

Nobody had asked him.

"My paper at Bram's is sixty-four blocks and the interest at a quarter, and it is written in blocks, because everything on that quay is written in blocks. If salt goes to a tenth of what it was in the spring, sixty-four blocks is a season's wage and a bit, and I clear it in one crossing, and Enna does not go on that paper at fourteen." He had not stopped working. "That is the whole of my interest and I have said it before and I will say it as often as anybody wants, and I would rather you all had it off me than worked it out about me."

"And your wage?" Sabra said. "Paid out of what?"

"Yes," Pell said. "I know."

Lyddan was twenty-two and Hanei was nineteen and neither of them said anything, and both of them had been digging since the morning without being told to, which was the whole of what they had to say and everybody under the tents heard it.

Sennet stood at the edge with his arms folded. "I would still want to know what happens at the Cold Well."

"Then say that," Ysoly said.

"I am saying it. It is not an answer."

"No."

They got her in at the last of the light and filled it, and it took less time to fill than it had taken to open, which is a thing about graves that none of them had known because none of them had ever dug one out here.

Hanei stood over it afterwards, and beside it, in the brown ground, four feet from the turned earth, was the print of a splayed foot the size of a dinner plate, standing there with the horn rim of it legible, because this was the only ground between the rims that kept anything.

"Master," she said. "What are you going to do?"

"Count the pound," Ysoly said. "Forty. Say it back."

"Forty."

"Say it to Onn at dusk and she says it to me and I cut it, and if you are ever wrong you come to me before somebody else comes to me." Ysoly went past her towards the wagons without stopping. "That is what I am going to do tonight, apprentice, and it is the only thing I have got that I am sure of."
