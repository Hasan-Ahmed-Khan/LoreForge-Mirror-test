They came in on the sixth evening with the glare going off the crust and the beacons throwing shadows for the first time since morning, and Hanei stood up on the box of the fourth wagon to see the water.

There was no water.

There was a quarter-mile of white ground with sixty stone collars set into it, and on every collar a hide lid held down under a ring of stones, and the lids had gone the colour of old rope and were split at the edges where the sun had had them. Numbers were cut into the collars, deep, and packed black. That was the whole of it.

She had carried a picture of this place since she was nine. The picture had a surface in it, and something moving on the surface.

"It's under," Pell said, going past on foot with a trace-chain over his shoulder. "Everything out here is under. Mask up, we're not in yet."

The station ran along the north edge: mud-brick cells, hide awnings poled out from the wall, a walled pound, and two rooms under a tiled roof with the Wardenry's board nailed beside the door. It smelled of rope and wet stone and, under both, of sulphur — the sour-egg breath that came up out of every shaft in the field and that nobody living here had smelled in years.

Between the post and the pound stood the taster's board. Slate, a man's height, in the wall's shade. Sixty numbers in three columns, nine of them ringed in chalk, and the day's date under the last column in a round careful hand.

Hanei read the nine twice and got them by heart, because they were the only numbers on the plain that had anything to do with drinking. Four. Eleven. Nineteen. Twenty-six. Thirty-four. Forty. Forty-three. Fifty. Fifty-eight.

Sabra Onn did not look at the board at all. She went past it with a tin cup in one hand and a bar under her arm, out into the field, and rolled the stones off the lid of the first collar in the first row.

The board's keeper came out from under the awning while the wagons were still coming up: a narrow man of about fifty with chalk to the second knuckle and a slate-rag through his belt.

"Rusk. I keep the board. Nine sweet this season, and the nine on it now were drawn and tasted at first light. Two blocks the crossing and I taste every barrel under your own eye. One block and you take the chalk."

"Neither," Ysoly said. She was already going down the string, touching the butts at the hoop as she came to them.

"Every line takes one or the other."

"Mine has a water-master."

Rusk looked out into the field, where an old woman was on her knees at collar one with her arm down a hole. "That'll take her till dark. There are fifty-one out there that are no good to anybody."

"She does all sixty," Ysoly said, and went on counting.

Five butts wet and twenty gallons in the sixth. Four hundred and twenty-five gallons had gone out of Bell Harrow on nine wagons; six days had drunk two hundred and fifty-five of them; what stood on the crust at the wells was a hundred and seventy gallons and a great deal of empty hide. She got to the ninth wagon, turned round, and counted them again.

Behind her the pound gate was open and the drovers were bringing the string in eight at a time. Forty-one head, all night, in shifts — rope over the collar, hide bucket down and up, the stone trough filled by hand and emptied by eight animals in about the time it took to fill it again.

"Apprentice," Sabra called, without turning round. "You're on the rope till it's light. Not the bucket."

"The rope till light, not the bucket."

"A bucket is a bucket. A rope is thirty feet of hemp over dressed stone, and if it goes you are not lifting anything out of that hole for the rest of the season." Sabra tipped the cup, spat, wiped the rim on her sleeve. "Six."

She capped six and moved to seven.

By full dark she had done thirty and the lamps were lit along the ring, and by the second shift she was working the far edge of the field with a lantern on the stone beside her: an old woman kneeling in the white with a tin cup, spitting.

At thirty-four she did not move on.

She drew a second cup and held it under the lantern and looked at it, which she had not done once in thirty-three shafts, and then took a mouthful and held it and spat that too, and sat back on her heels with her hands on her knees, and stayed there long enough that Hanei, sixty yards off on the rope, stopped paying out and had to be shouted at.

Then Sabra got up, put the lid on, rolled the stones back onto it herself instead of leaving them, and walked in.
