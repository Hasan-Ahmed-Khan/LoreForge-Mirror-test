Vask did his arithmetic standing up, because there was nowhere at the Centre to sit that was not wet.

"I will be quick, and then I will stop, because a man who goes on after he is complete has stopped believing his own figures." He had the book open on his forearm. "The paper on your wagons falls due at the turn and is held two doors from my counting-house by people who have done as I asked for thirty-four years. It can be taken up on Thursday. The sum owing you at retirement is a promise from a body whose whole book is priced in salt, and if there is a working well at this cairn you will not get it, and neither will I. It can be paid in the autumn instead of owed. And a season's wage to each of the eleven. That was the fourth term at the Dry Ferry and it has not moved."

"And the fifth term," Ysoly said.

"Stands. Corrow's paper at Bram's, taken up and cancelled, and it does not turn on anything he does or says, and it did not at the Ferry either." Vask did not look at Pell while he said it, which was the one courtesy left on that ground. "I put that term in a book at Bell Harrow before your tile was fired, madam. I am not going to stand at the centre of the plain and pretend it is a thing I am offering you tonight."

Pell Corrow sat down on a spoil ring and put his hands on his knees and said nothing whatever.

"Now the part you want and will not like." Vask turned the book round, though it was too dark to read it. "The proviso says the survey be begun. Not finished, not reported, not certified. Begun. A man who walks out here in the autumn, sets one instrument on that ground and drives one stake has begun a survey, and there is no clause in the Article saying when he must be done. Ferrant's was never begun at all, which is the only reason anybody at a rim has ever noticed it." He shut the book. "You will hear that in a rim court in four years from somebody paid to say it, and I would rather you had it from a man who—"

He did not finish it.

"The requirement goes on the roll," Denet said. "Beside the seal, in her hand, at Bell Harrow, within forty days. A line of writing in a book that is read, naming the ground and the water and the requirer and the date. It is the only line that will ever exist about this place, because there is no fair copy of my survey anywhere in the world and there is not going to be." He was looking at Vask. "You bought that. Six lines, south, out of the depot's bag. I am not complaining of it. I am saying what is left."

Iseul Fane came up from the cairn with the cloth in her hand.

She unwrapped it in front of them and let the cloth fall over her wrist. The lead had been in it since the year one hundred and ninety-one and had taken the shape of the folds: a disc the width of a palm, dull, numbered on the roll at Bell Harrow, wired at the rim to an iron pin the length of a forearm. Every hand on the Tamm Line had watched her carry that saddlebag for twenty-three days.

"Two witnesses that can be named. Warden's practice is to name them first and I will keep to it. Oreth Vask, factor of the Salt Assembly at Bell Harrow. Sabra Onn, water-master, of Bell Harrow. You will be named on the roll and you may be called at either rim. If either of you refuses it now I will find two others and it will take me an hour."

Neither of them refused it.

"Not the surveyor, because a finder should not witness his own finding. Not the master, because she is a party." Iseul looked at Ysoly a moment. "And because in about four minutes she is going to need those two standing where they are."

She walked to the edge of the ground six paces west of the hole, where the brown was still hard, set the pin, and drove it with the flat of a spade through the crust to the collar, and it went in the way nothing on the plain goes in.

Then she stood up and put her hands at her sides.

"I have been twelve years at the floor of this Article and I have never once had to know what I would do." She was not speaking as an officer of anything, and every person there heard it. "The Wardens will hold this ground until the hazard is surveyed and lifted. I have read what that has meant once already, at a shaft forty miles out of a rim, to a man who asked twice and died asking. I am going to speak it anyway, and I would like it understood that I know exactly what I am doing."

Then she spoke it, and her voice went back to the register she read statutes in, which was flat and unhurried and belonged to nobody.

"Water rising or standing within the bounds is a hazard to the crossings and is held in trust by the Wardenry. Where such water is found, a Warden of the Third Rank or above, standing upon the ground and speaking before two witnesses, may declare the ground about it a hazard, and set a seal upon it, and hold it against all comers until the hazard is surveyed and lifted."

She set the disc against the pin and turned the wire down twice.

"I am Iseul Fane, Warden of the Third Rank, sealed to it at Bell Harrow in the year one hundred and ninety-one. I stand upon the ground at Benchmark One, within the bounds by the First Article, the salt lying to thirty-one inches at the thinnest of eleven bores. I declare this ground a hazard and I set the seal upon it, before Oreth Vask and before Sabra Onn, on the twenty-third day of the summer season of the year two hundred and three."

Nobody moved. On the northern horizon the fires of thirty-six wagons stood up in a line, nine miles off, and would be here at first light.

"The Wardenry shall pay the finder the worth of the ground at the last assessed price of salt." Iseul straightened up from the pin. "The finder is Denet Rasca, Warden-surveyor, sealed of the College of Levels, who augered eleven bores at this cairn in the seasons of two hundred and one to two hundred and three and floated a College glass on nine of them."

Vask's head came round.

"Yes," Iseul said. "The Wardenry owes itself. Nobody at Bell Harrow has ever had to write that sentence and they will have to write it in forty days, because I am going to carry it to them. And it is the only way a number of his gets onto any roll anywhere, and he knows it, and I will not pretend I did not think of that."

Denet Rasca stood at the second stake with his hands at his sides and did not say one word.

Then Iseul Fane turned round to face Ysoly Tamm across six feet of trodden brown ground, and her voice changed again, and it was the licence office at the head of the seventh quay with the tiles cooling on the rack behind her.

"Master Tamm. Under the Second Article, before one witness that can be named — Sabra Onn, water-master — the cause is this. On the twentieth and twenty-first days of this season you opened water within the bounds of the Meniscus without a Warden to open it, against the Third Article, by your own order and in your own presence, and you have this day drawn one thousand and eighty gallons out of it. The cause will be entered beside your roll number at Bell Harrow and you may sue it at the rim."

She put her hand out, palm up, into the space between them.

"The tile is broken in the holder's hand and in her sight. I do not take it off you. Hold it up."

Ysoly Tamm put her fist round four inches of fired clay on a thong at her neck, and thirty years of salt, and eleven people, and two hundred more nine miles behind her.

She lifted it.

Iseul's fingers closed over the back of her hand. Neither of them was holding anything yet, and the whole of the Tamm Line stood in the mire around a hole with a lead disc on a pin at the edge of it.
