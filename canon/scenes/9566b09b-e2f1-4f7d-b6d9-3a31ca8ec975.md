The Warden had been standing at the rim of the hole since first light, and she had not once put a boot inside the ring of spoil.

Ysoly Tamm came out to her at the second hour with the licence book under her arm and did not open it. Behind them the string stood roped and yoked and had stood roped and yoked for an hour, because nobody had told it to go and nobody had told it to stop.

"I will take it in order," Iseul Fane said. "Who ordered it dug."

"I did."

"Name the ones who dug it."

"Lyddan. Bris. The apprentice. Both Kaddals. Rooe on the buckets after midnight, Sennet on the steps, and Vesh went down twice and came up again because his knee is gone." Ysoly said it flat and whole. "I stood on the rim and told them when to come out."

"With what."

"Two spades off wagon seven and a hide bucket off the surveyor's rig."

"To what depth."

"Nine feet and a hand, four foot square, because a round hole in soft ground closes on you and a square one tells you when it is going." She looked down into it. "There were two feet of water in it at the third hour of the night and there are two feet in it now. It has stopped coming up and it has not gone down."

Iseul looked at that for a while, the way a person looks at a figure that has been added twice.

Then she said, "'Every well within the bounds is the Wardenry's to open, to close and to ration.' Third Article, original to the year one. I read you the whole of it across a table at Sixty Wells with your water-master standing behind you, and you told me to say the part I was not saying, and I said it."

"It is a hole."

"It is nine feet deep, it has water standing in it, and a woman drank out of it at dawn."

Sennet had come round the far side of the spoil ring with his arms folded, which was how he said everything. "There is a difference between a hole and a well, Warden."

"Name it."

"A well has a collar on it. Sixty Wells has sixty of them, cut and packed black. The Cold Well has dressed stone twenty feet down and a windlass house over the top and a man with a padlock."

"Then a well is a thing somebody paid for," Iseul said, "and the Wardenry's authority runs to the water that cost money and stops at the water that did not."

Nobody had an answer to that, and Denet Rasca, who had been standing at the second stake since before Ysoly came out, said, "It runs the other way as well. You should have it both ways before you decide."

"Say it."

"If that is a well, then every hole any man ever puts in this ground is yours to open and close, from here to either rim and for ever, and the Wardenry has this morning acquired eight hundred miles of wells that do not exist yet." He stopped and began the sentence again, which he did. "I'm sorry. And if it is not a well, then there is nothing in the statute, or in the Compact, or in any counting-house at either rim that stops the next man digging one twenty paces off, and the one after him, and there is no instrument anywhere for making either of them stop."

"Yes," Iseul said, and that was the whole of what she said about it.

She turned to face the wagons, so that the hands under the awning line could hear her without appearing to listen, which was where all of them were.

"Master Tamm. I have cause to revoke your licence, and I am going to say so on the ground, in front of your people, this morning, while there is nothing standing on it yet. A thing that can be done to you in a week without warning is not law. It is weather." She let that lie. "You opened water within the bounds without a Warden to open it. It is not a doubtful cause and there is no reading of the Third Article that saves you from it, and I want you to have heard me say it in the daylight rather than work it out at a fire."

"Then say the rest," Ysoly said.

"There is no rest yet."

Hanei, at the guy-rope end with her hands still black from the clay, said, "The packet," and then went the colour of a fired tile, because she had not meant to say anything at all.

Iseul put her hand inside her coat and took out the waxed sleeve, which she had carried four years without opening and had now opened on two days running, and every hand on the Tamm Line watched her do it a third time.

"'Provided also, that the seal lie upon the ground and the water and upon nothing else. It shall not reach to survey, level, note, log nor instrument, nor to the person carrying the same, nor compel the showing of them. Papers are the goods of him that holds them, and go off the ground in his hand.'" She folded the sleeve and put it away. "Whatever I do here, I cannot take a leaf of it, I cannot open it, and I cannot make any of you show me what is in it. That is the whole of what I have to give you and I am giving it to you before anybody asks."

"And the water?"

"Until I speak, the water is yours and you may draw it. After I speak it is held in trust and a cup out of that hole is against the Wardenry, and I will act on it, and I will be sorry." Iseul looked north up the beacon line, where there was nothing, and would be something in two days. "I will speak it before the joint tile comes through. I am telling you that so that nobody here can say afterwards that they were not told."

She went to fetch her animal.

Ysoly Tamm stood a moment at the rim of a hole with the licence book under her arm. Then she went down the string with her hand out, touching every butt at the hoop as she came to it. Seventeen wet. Nineteen dry and stopped.

She got to the ninth wagon, turned round, and counted them again.
