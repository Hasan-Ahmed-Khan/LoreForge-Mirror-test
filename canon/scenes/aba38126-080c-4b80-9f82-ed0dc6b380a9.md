The heat came off it at four, and Sennet said the thing everybody had been holding.

"It isn't salt."

"Say the rest of it," Ysoly said.

"The First Article says the bounds are ground below the old shoreline and bearing salt to the depth of a man's arm. I have heard that read out at a weighbridge twice." Sennet had his arms folded, which was how he said everything. "This is brown and it takes a bootprint and it gives like ash. It is not the crust. If it is not within the bounds, the Fourth Article does not reach it — not the seal, not the hold, none of it — and the Article says what the Wardenry answers for beyond the bounds, which is nothing."

"That can be argued," Denet said.

"Then argue it."

"It can be argued in good faith by a rim advocate who has never been out here, and no court at either rim has ever ruled on the bounds, and it would take about four years." He was already moving. "And it is wrong. I know it is wrong because I am the man who proved it wrong, before I knew what I was proving."

He knelt at the nearest spoil ring and dug his hand into it and came up with a fistful.

"The brown goes down about a hand. It is clay worked up into the salt by the wet underneath, and that is why it gives. Under the brown it is this." He held it out — grey-white, coarse, and it ran off his palm the way it ran off a blade at the kilns. "Salt, and it goes on being salt past the elbow, and the grey clay does not begin until below the arm. Eleven bores, eleven spoil rings, still heaped where I left them two summers ago, and any of you can kneel down and rub one between finger and thumb without taking my word for anything. The thinnest of the eleven ran salt to thirty-one inches." He let it fall. "That is over the arm by any reckoning of the arm anybody has ever made. This ground is inside the bounds, in my own hand, and it is the only piece of work I have ever done that I wish I had done badly."

Iseul Fane had come up while he was on his knees and had not interrupted him, which was itself a kind of statement.

"That is a correct reading of the First Article," she said.

"I know."

"Warden," said Lyddan, who was twenty-two and in his second season, "when they seal us—"

"Stamp." It came out of her without a break in it, the way it had come out of her at a licence office window in Bell Harrow twenty days ago. "A stamp is permission and a seal is a hold. Your master is stamped. Nothing on this crossing has been—"

She stopped.

It went round the whole of them in the time it takes to draw a breath.

"The Fourth Article," Iseul said, "was sealed in the year one hundred and sixty-seven, after the Brackening. It is the only amendment the statute has ever had."

Then she recited it, standing on brown ground with her hands at her sides: water rising or standing within the bounds is a hazard and is held in trust by the Wardenry; a Warden of the Third Rank or above, upon the ground, speaking before two witnesses; forty days to the roll at Bell Harrow or it is no seal from the day it was set; the seal to lie upon the ground and the water and upon nothing else, not upon survey, level, note, log nor instrument, nor upon the person carrying the same; the finder to be paid the worth of the ground at the last assessed price of salt.

And then the last of them.

"'Provided lastly, that where the master of a lawful crossing stands upon sealed ground, holding his tile whole, he may require of the Warden, before the same two witnesses, that the survey be begun before the turn of the season next following; and the Warden shall enter the requirement upon the roll beside the seal. A requirement so made is not undone by the after-breaking of the tile. But a tile broken before the speaking of it is no licence, and he that holds no licence may require nothing. And if the season turn and no survey be begun, the seal is lifted of itself, and the ground is free.'"

Nobody moved.

Then Iseul Fane put her hand inside her coat and took out the waxed sleeve, and opened it, and read the Fourth Article again to herself with her lips not moving, and put it away.

She had carried that sleeve for four years without needing it, and every hand on the Tamm Line had seen her carry it, and every one of them watched her open it.

"I have got it right," she said. "I wanted to be certain."

"Are you certain of the rest?" Ysoly said.

"No." Iseul looked out at eleven stakes. "The Article says where such water is found. It does not say by whom, and it does not say when, and it has never once had to. What I have found at this cairn is eleven holes a man augered two summers ago and a bucket of wet clay I drew with my own hands." She said it evenly, and it cost her something to say it evenly. "I will not speak a sentence on this ground that I cannot square with what is on it."

"Then we'll dig you a hole," Ysoly said.

It went very quiet.

"Master—"

"The joint tile is four days behind and comes through here on the twenty-fourth. Thirty-six wagons, two hundred people, a cook wagon and a taster, and Vask on it or I have never known him. Somebody with two hundred mouths to feed puts a spade in this ground because a drover told him a story at a fire, and there is nobody standing here with a tile at their neck when it happens." Ysoly had the thong out and the fired clay in her fist, and she did not hold it up. "Then it goes the way Ferrant went. Sealed in a morning, capped with plain-salt and mortar, surveyor appointed when somebody gets round to it, and a man asking twice and dying asking.

"So we dig it. Here, in front of the Warden, before they come, with every one of you on the ground and my tile whole at my neck. That is not a count and nobody has to like it."

Sabra Onn said, "It costs a day."

"It costs a day and we have three."

"We have three to the Cold Well and the joint tile is four out. You cannot stand here for four days on three days' grace."

"No," Ysoly agreed. "Vesh. Rooe. A hundred blocks off wagons three, five and eight, laid off square on the ground where the glare will find them, and every one of them counted onto the stick before it comes off the axle."

Nobody argued. Vesh took his hat off and put it back on, which was the whole of what Vesh had to say about it, and went to wagon three.

Ballast came off the Tamm Line for the first time in thirty years — four thousand pounds of second-grade Assembly salt, pressed to the Compact mould, owed at the far rim at the Assembly's own assessed price by the master personally and not by the firm — and went up in a stack beside the cairn, white on brown, and by the fifth course nobody could look at it without the mask.

Ysoly turned the tally past heads, past water drawn, to the ballast face, and cut a hundred where the hands could watch her do it. Then she turned it to the fourth face and cut it again.
