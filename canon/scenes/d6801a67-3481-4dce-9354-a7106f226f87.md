Ysoly's cell was a mud-brick box in the ring with a bench, a lamp, and a hide across the doorway. She was still up, the licence book open in front of her and nothing written in it.

Sabra put the hide back. "Send for the packet."

"Sabra—"

"Send for the packet."

Ysoly looked at the two of them for the length of a breath, then went to the doorway and called for Hanei; and Hanei came in off the rope with her palms raw, went to the fourth wagon herself and lifted her own blanket off it, and carried it in with both hands the way she had carried it out.

Pell Corrow came in behind her without being asked. There was nowhere on nine wagons to be sent, and Ysoly did not try to send him.

Denet set the packet on the bench under the lamp. Oiled hide the size of a folded coat, waxed thread through it in a running stitch, two grey discs of lead struck flat over the thread at the head, the corners worn pale.

"Once it is cut it can never be made to look as it looks now, and I want that said in front of all of you, because in about a minute somebody here is going to wish it had not been. After tonight this is a bag of paper, worth whatever the man holding it can persuade you it is worth."

"Then don't," Ysoly said.

"I have to."

"You have had six days on my wagons to have to."

"I have had five months," Denet said. "And six days. And this afternoon, and the twenty-two blocks."

He took out a small knife and cut the thread at the head of the stitching, one pull. The lead came away on the thread in his hand, both discs together, and he laid them on the bench beside the lamp where everybody could see them lying loose.

Then he counted the leaves out, saying the number of each as he set it down. They were not clean — field sheets, ruled in pencil and inked over afterwards, foxed at the edges, one with a thumbprint in the corner in something that was not ink. The last was folded in four and larger than the rest.

Forty-one.

"Read me one," Ysoly said.

He took a sheet without choosing it. "'Bore nine. Two hundred and forty paces east of Benchmark One. Auger stopped eleven feet four on wet grey clay. Sample floated at' — the figure is here and it will mean nothing to you — 'against reference. Fresh.'" He put it down. "Nine of the eleven bores came up wet and every wet one read fresh. Two were dry, and I have written down that they were dry, because a man who leaves the dry ones out is not a surveyor."

"Who wrote it?"

"I did."

"Say the rest."

"The Wardenry sent me to re-level the centre routes after the crust moved. Nobody asked me for borings. I put them in because the brown ground gave under my boot, and it has given under everybody's boot for two hundred years, and every one of us wrote rotten crust in a notebook and walked round it. Wet ground out there is brine. It is the first thing a driver is taught, it is correct, and it has been correct every time anybody tested it — which is never, because you do not test the thing that kills you." He straightened the pile with two fingers. "I did not find anything. It was lying in the open. I floated a glass on it and it took about a minute."

Under the mud brick the pound was still working. One to eight. One to eight.

"Where is the other one," Ysoly said.

"There isn't one."

"Colleges keep rolls."

"The rule is that a finding goes on the roll before it goes to the body that paid for it. Four lines — surveyor, ground, date, one line of result. It would have been at Tallow Reach by autumn, and there would be a clerk eight hundred miles from here who could swear to what I found, and I could have stopped carrying it." He was looking at the leaves and not at her. "I drafted it twice. I sent neither. I stitched the bag and struck both seals over the thread. It is the only rule I have ever broken, and I broke it by doing nothing, which is how I do most things."

"So there is one of it. On my wagons."

"Yes. And if a lamp goes over on it there is nothing — no copy, no entry, no clerk's abstract. My memory of my own arithmetic, said out loud in a room, which is evidence nowhere on this plain." He put his hand flat on the bench beside the pile without touching it. "That is what it is worth to the people who will want it. Not what it says. That this is the only place it says it."

Pell Corrow, in the corner, had not stitched anything in some minutes.

Ysoly Tamm looked at the forty-one leaves and thought about a yard of rim-oak under her coat with four faces on it and no second one anywhere in the world, on which was written what every hand on her line was owed and what their families got if they did not come back.

"You asked for me by name," she said. "Four blocks of first grade for one man and his own water, when the Wardenry's own relay would have carried you south for nothing in nine days. Why."

"Because you are eleven people, and I could not think of a smaller number that gets a wagon to the centre."

Nobody said anything to that for a while. Hanei, by the doorway with her raw hands behind her, understood that she had heard a thing said in front of her that would be true for the rest of her life, and that nobody in the room had thought about her being there when it was said.

"One more, and I would rather say it than have somebody find it." Denet did not look up. "'Where such water is found, a Warden of the Third Rank or above, standing upon the ground and speaking before two witnesses, may declare the ground about it a hazard, and set a seal upon it, and hold it against all comers.' Fourth Article. It cannot be done by letter and it cannot be done from the rim. Somebody of that rank has to stand on the ground and say it aloud."

"Fane," said Pell, from the corner. It was the first word he had said since the article that afternoon.

"She is the only one of that rank between the rims while this crossing is out, and the next nearest is three hundred and eighty-five miles behind us. I have spent six days walking the instrument of the Fourth Article towards the one place on the plain it must never stand on. She is asleep in the post about ninety paces from here, and she has been perfectly decent to me for eleven years."

"Then we tell her," Ysoly said.

The cell was very quiet.

"Nobody says anything. Four people in a room and a girl in the doorway, and I say we tell the Warden, and not one of you opens your mouth."

Sabra Onn said, "No."

"Say it properly."

"We do not tell the Warden."

Ysoly looked round all of them, one at a time, taking her time about it. Then she reached over and turned the lamp down, which was the nearest thing to an answer anybody got.
