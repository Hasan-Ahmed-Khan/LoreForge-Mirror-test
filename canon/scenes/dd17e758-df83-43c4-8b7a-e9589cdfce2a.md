The northbound crossing was hitching at first light, eighteen wagons and a great deal of shouting, and the noise of it came in at the depot door while Denet Rasca stood at the table and said yes.

"Yes. Four hundred blocks. I will sign it."

Vask had the pen already inked.

"One condition, and I'm sorry — I should say it is not a haggle. I would take three hundred, and two, and I would take the water off this line's account and nothing else at all." Denet had his hands behind his back like a man being examined. "The finding goes on the College roll. Four lines. Surveyor, ground, date, one line of result. And the sale entered beside it in your name, so that anybody at the far rim in a hundred years can see that the Assembly bought it and what it cost."

The pen did not move.

"No," Vask said.

"It is four lines in a book eight hundred miles from anywhere."

"It is a book that is read." He set the pen down across the inkstand, squarely, the way he did everything. "You are asking me to buy a thing and then let it be announced. Forward salt does not care who owns the water, surveyor. It does not care whether it is capped or dug or held in trust. It cares that there is water. Four hundred blocks buys silence. It does not buy an entry."

"Then I can't."

"You are throwing away four hundred blocks and this woman's roof for a clerical formality."

"Yes," Denet said. "I'm sorry — I should say that I would like to sign it, that I have wanted to since the stair last night, and that I am not doing this because I am brave. It is the rule I have already broken once, and I broke it by doing nothing, and doing nothing is available to me again this morning and I can feel it standing there. If the number does not go on the roll, it never happened. That is not a formality, factor. That is the only thing a level is."

Ysoly Tamm stood by the door with her licence book under her arm and did not say one word, and it cost her more than anything she had done in thirty years on the salt.

"Very well," Vask said, and drew the north bag and the south bag across the table.

He wrote the first letter in about four minutes, and did not hurry any of it, and folded it and sealed it and held it up.

"North, with the crossing outside, to my own counting-house at Bell Harrow. It concerns the paper held on your wagons, which falls due at the turn, and which is held two doors from my office by people who do as I ask because I have asked them for thirty-four years." He put it in the north bag. "I would rather be the man who mentioned it than the man who did not."

He took a second sheet.

"South, in the depot's bag, to the keeper of the roll at the Level House at Tallow Reach. It says that Denet Rasca, sealed of the College of Levels, ran a survey of the plain's centre in the seasons of two hundred and one to two hundred and three, sealed his findings in the spring of this year, entered nothing on the roll, and has carried the packet on his person since. Six lines, and not one thing in them that is untrue, which I am glad of, because I have never been good at the other."

Denet did not move.

"Your College will lift the seal — its own statutes leave it no choice, and you know them better than I do. And forty-one leaves in the hand of a man who is not a sealed surveyor are not a survey. They are a story about water, told by somebody with a reason to tell it. I will not have to buy them, or burn them, or take them off you, and the Article could not have taken them either." He sealed it. "The south bag goes on the joint tile in four days. It will be at the Level House four days after you are. I am telling you that because you would rather know, and because a man who does not tell you is hoping you will hurry and make a mistake."

He put it in the south bag and closed the flap.

"Warden Fane sleeps at the relay eighty paces from this table," he said. "She is of the Third Rank, she is the only one between the rims while you are out, she has been told nothing at all by anybody, and the reason for that is—"

He stopped, and opened the book, and made a small mark somewhere near the top of the page.

"Well," he said.

He shook Ysoly's hand at the depot door, because he had shaken her hand on a quay in the smoke for nineteen years, and it was not a lie then either.

Iseul Fane rode past the head of the string at first light the way she had ridden past the head of a string for twelve years, and then stopped, which she did not do.

"Master. There is a factor of the Salt Assembly on this rock who was standing in a yard at Bell Harrow the morning I stamped your tile. He came out on four hask with no wagon, at fifty-eight, and he has been in that room three days waiting for nine wagons." Iseul did not look at the depot while she said it. "I wrote your endorsement myself — one man, his own water, no freight above what he carries — and I have thought about that line for thirteen days. Is there anything on your way-bill that is not on your way-bill?"

"No," Ysoly said.

Iseul Fane sat her animal a moment longer. "Two days to the Lees. The Wardenry will have your stage endorsed there."

They loaded through the morning and rolled at second light, which is what a crossing does when it has been up all night and cannot afford the day.

Ysoly went down the string with her hand out. Twenty-eight butts full. Ten gallons in the twenty-ninth. Seven dry and stopped — eight hundred and fifty gallons of hard brack out of a rock cistern, two hundred and forty-six cakes of pressed glasswort on top of it, and the wagons sat down on their axles like animals lying down. More water than the Tamm Line had ever rolled out of anywhere in thirty years.

She got to the ninth wagon, turned round, and counted them again.

Then she took the tally out from under her coat and turned it to the debt face at the tailgate of the first wagon, where the hands could watch her do it, and cut eleven.

"Twenty," Sabra Onn said.

"There is no face for twenty."

"No," Sabra agreed.

Ysoly put the knife back in her coat.

Sabra walked to the head of the string and said it to nobody, to the rock, the way she said it every dawn so that afterwards no one could claim it had not been said.

"Two gallons the head, half a gallon the hask. Eleven and forty-one. Forty-two and a half the day. Seventeen days to water."

Nobody answered her. Nobody ever did.

Pell Corrow came up the line last with a needle in his hat and a girth over his shoulder, got onto the box of the fourth wagon, and said something about the near-side leader on eight; and Bris answered him about the leader on eight and about nothing else, and that was how it was going to be.

Hanei put her blanket over a pound and thirteen ounces of stitched hide in the locker under the seat, and got up beside him.

The stone let go of the wheels and the crust took them back, tick and drop and tick, and by the second beacon the Dry Ferry was a grey line with a hide roof on it — and behind them the shutter above the depot stood open the way it had stood open for three days, and a man who had never in his life walked further than the length of a quay sat behind it with a book and four days to wait.
