# 1. The Last Loading

> Bell Harrow, RR 203. Ysoly Tamm assembles her last crossing on the stepped quays: water weighed at the weighbridge against the Compact's ten-pound cylinder, a licence tile fired and hung at her neck, two hundred and twelve blocks of Assembly ballast stacked low between the axles, nine wagons, forty-one hask, eleven hands. Sabra Onn argues four days' grace out of her; Oreth Vask prices the crossing without finishing a sentence; Pell Corrow pays down his debt to Thecla Bram in news, because news is cheaper than salt. On the last morning a Warden-surveyor named Denet Rasca comes aboard with a College-sealed glass and a hide packet stitched shut under two seals that can only be struck once. Nobody will open it. Ysoly enters it on the way-bill as passenger's goods, gives Hanei charge of it, and the bell rings the Tamm Line out nine times.

## Weighed and Not Measured

The weighbridge stood on the third step, in among the kilns, so that everything weighed at Bell Harrow was weighed in smoke.

Hanei came up the quay stairs behind the first four butts with her instructions repeated back word for word — the stairs and not the ramp, and don't let the hide touch a kiln wall — and thought that people made too much of this work. Empty, a butt was nothing: forty pounds of oiled hide, rope and a hoop of rim-oak.

Then the weighmaster filled one, and it took four men to walk it onto the platform, and the beam went over with the sound of a door shutting in another room.

"Three hundred pounds," said Sabra Onn. "Say it back."

"Three hundred pounds."

"Thirty gallons the butt. Ten pounds the gallon. It has been ten pounds the gallon since the Compact and it will be ten pounds the gallon when they put me under. Say the whole of it."

"Thirty gallons the butt, ten pounds the gallon, three hundred pounds the butt."

Sabra went on down the line without looking at her. At the end of the beam, chained through a ring set in the stone, lay a plain iron cylinder about the length of a forearm. Hanei had seen a dozen of them without once looking at one — in the counting-houses, in the licence office, in the depot where the tenements bought their rope. Ten pounds. The most copied object in the world, and nobody could tell you who cast the first.

Past the platform the step ended, and the town ended, and the Meniscus began: white to the curve of the eye, with the kiln smoke lying flat across the top of it, because there was no wind before noon and would not be.

Ysoly Tamm came up at the eighth butt with the licence book under her arm, its corners gone soft as cloth. She did not look out at the plain. Hanei had noticed that in eleven days: she looked at wheels, at hide, at hands, at the beam of a scale, and never once out.

"Figures," Ysoly said.

"Two gallons the head, half a gallon the hask," Sabra said. "Eleven and forty-one."

"Twenty-two and twenty and a half. Forty-two and a half the day."

"Six days to the wells."

"Two hundred and fifty-five gallons for the leg," Ysoly said. "Three days' grace on top. Three hundred and eighty-two and a half."

"Five days' grace."

Ysoly closed the book on her finger. "Five."

"Five."

"That is eighty-five gallons over what is customary. Eight hundred and fifty pounds. Twenty-one blocks off the load, and the blocks are the only thing on those wagons anybody pays me for. Twenty-one blocks is the wheelwright and the quarter on the paper."

"Two hundred people died six days south of where you are standing," Sabra said, "on water a man had chalked sweet the morning before."

"I know what happened at the wells."

"You know it. I was in it."

Down on the second step a kiln crew was chanting a block out of its mould. The weighmaster stood with his chalk and looked at neither of them, which was its own sort of listening.

"Four days," Ysoly said.

Sabra worked it without moving her lips. "Four hundred and twenty-five gallons."

"Four hundred and twenty-five. Fourteen butts full and five in the fifteenth. Four thousand two hundred and fifty pounds of water on nine wagons, and twenty-one butts riding empty and stopped."

The weighmaster wrote it up. Under the Third Article the licence carried one figure only, and it was not that one: eleven heads, two gallons the head, six days to the next licensed water. A hundred and thirty-two gallons — the floor, and the whole of what the Wardenry weighed.

"And the forty-one?" Hanei said.

"Not heads." Ysoly did not look up from the book. "The Compact settled the word in the year forty-seven. A head is a person named on a licence. The law does not weigh a hask and never has."

"Then who does?"

"I do," said Sabra.

When the last butt came off the platform Ysoly walked the line and counted, touching each at the hoop, and got to fifteen. Then she walked back and counted them again. Nobody said anything about it. Hanei gathered that nobody ever did.

"Cut it," Sabra said.

"It's in the book."

"Cut it on the stick, where they can see you." Sabra tipped her chin at the hands sitting along the wall out of the smoke, waiting to be told to lift something. "The book is yours. The stick is theirs."

So Ysoly took the tally out from under her coat — a yard of rim-oak gone black, four-sided — turned it to the water face, and cut four long notches and a stub, and eleven people watched her do it.

## The Rack of Tiles

The licence office was two rooms at the head of the seventh quay, and it smelled of clay dust, hot wax, and the vinegar they cleaned the stamps with. Through the window there was nothing at all: below the wall of the last step cut, the crust ran out flat and white for eight hundred miles, to a town Ysoly had walked to thirty times and could not have found on a map.

Iseul Fane had the way-bill squared in front of her and a pen she did not hurry.

"Tamm. Nine wagons, forty-one draught, eleven hands."

"Eleven."

"Named."

Ysoly read them off without the book, because a master who needed the book for eleven names had no business with nine wagons. Sabra Onn. Pell Corrow. Vesh, Ottery, the two Kaddals. Rooe. Sennet. Lyddan. Bris. "Hanei," she finished.

"Hanei," said Hanei, from the bench by the door, before she could stop herself.

Iseul wrote it. She sat like a woman who had been told once, long ago, how to sit at a table with a statute on it. "Route."

"The centre line. Bell Harrow to Tallow Reach on the beacons, by the wells, the Ferry and the Cold Well."

"Season."

"Summer, this year."

Iseul went into the second room and came back with the tile.

It was four inches square and heavier than four inches of anything ought to be, white with the salt-ash glaze, and the stamp had gone into the clay before the firing so that the roll number stood under the glaze rather than on it — a thing you could not scratch off, could not counterfeit at any rim, and could not mend.

"That is the Wardenry's," Iseul said, "until the far rim. You carry it at the neck. You do not leave it in a wagon and you do not hand it to a hand."

Hanei leaned forward off the bench. "And when the Warden seals it—"

"Stamps it." Iseul did not stop writing. "A stamp is permission. A seal is a hold. You are stamped, or you are sealed. Nothing in the statute has ever been both, and the day somebody tells you it is both, you will want to have known the difference beforehand."

"What happens if it breaks?"

"'A broken tile is no licence.'" Iseul turned the way-bill round for signing. "Second Article. There is nothing else in it about them."

"It does not break," Ysoly said, "because it lives on the thong, and it never comes off the thong, and nobody ever asks me to hold it up so they can look at it. That is the whole trade. Learn that part and you can forget the article."

She signed. Iseul turned the sheet back, laid a second sheet beside it, and set her finger on a line at the foot.

"One endorsement. A passenger."

"I did not take a passenger."

"He has paid the fee at this office and the fee is entered." Iseul read it as written, the way she read everything. "Denet Rasca. Warden-surveyor, sealed of the College of Levels. Bell Harrow to the far rim, one man, his own water, no freight above what he carries."

"A Warden-surveyor."

"Yes."

"There is a joint tile behind me in the yard," Ysoly said. "Thirty-six wagons, two hundred people, a cook wagon and a taster, going out four days behind me over the same beacons. Why is a Warden-surveyor buying passage off the smallest line on this quay?"

"He asked for a nine-wagon line."

"Asked for."

"By name," Iseul said. "Yours."

The fee was four blocks of first grade, which was more than a passenger was worth and less than Ysoly could afford to refuse, and neither of them said so. She took the pen back and endorsed him.

"And you," she said, still writing. "Third Rank, riding out ahead of nine wagons and eleven hands, when there are two hundred people going the same road in four days."

"The Wardenry assigns."

"That is not an answer."

"No," Iseul agreed, and drew the sheet away and turned it face down to dry.

On the wall of the second room the tiles for the season stood in a rack on edge, ticking faintly as they cooled, and at the far end of the rack were the ones nobody had come for. Ysoly could read two of the roll numbers from where she stood, and she had drunk with one of them at the Ferry the year before last.

"Those go back to the clay in the spring," Iseul said, without turning round.

Ysoly put the thong over her head and let the tile down inside her shirt, where it sat cold against the breastbone for a while and then stopped being cold, the way it always did. By the time she was down the quay stairs she had stopped feeling it at all, which was the whole trouble with the thing and always had been.

## Two Hundred and Twelve

The salt came up from the counting-house on the quay in barrows, six blocks to a barrow, and went into the wagons low and flat between the axles where it would do the work it was really for.

Ysoly walked the line and watched the stacking, because a block laid high was a block that would walk the wagon sideways in a crosswind on day nineteen, and the men laying them knew that, and she watched anyway. Second grade, pressed to the Compact mould, forty pounds each, every one of them assayed and priced and owned by somebody who would never see the far rim.

"Two hundred and twelve," said the counting-house clerk, at the end of it.

"Two hundred and twelve," Ysoly said, and cut it on the ballast face.

Eight thousand four hundred and eighty pounds of salt. Four thousand two hundred and fifty of water. A little under a ton and a half the wagon before the fodder went on, on level crust, under four hasks apiece, and it would walk. It would walk out of Bell Harrow and it would walk into Tallow Reach, and at no point in the forty-one days between would one pound of it belong to her.

Pell Corrow was sitting on the tailgate of the fourth wagon with a trace-chain across his knees and a hammer, talking. He had been talking since the barrows started.

"—and I says to him, it's second grade because your father graded it, and he didn't take that the way I'd hoped, and now I've got to get my rope somewhere else, which is a pity, because the man's rope is the only honest thing about him. Master. Your near-side leader on eight has got a hot foot."

"Which one?"

"The old one. Long." He set the chain down. "Not lame. Hot. I'd walk her on the spare line to the wells."

"Do that."

"That's what I'm doing." He picked the chain up again.

Oreth Vask came into the yard at the middle of the morning with a book and no coat, because the yard was too hot for coats, and he stopped first at the fourth wagon.

"Corrow. Is Enna reading yet?"

"She's eleven, sir. She reads better than I do."

"That is not a high wall," Vask said pleasantly, "but neither is mine," and went on up the line to Ysoly, and by the time he reached her he had the book open.

"Two hundred and twelve blocks," he said. "The Assembly's, out of the counting-house, at the last assessed grade, to the weighbridge at Tallow Reach against your bill and your tally. The rate stands where it stood."

"The rate has stood where it stood for four seasons and the kiln price has moved twice."

"The kiln price is not the assessed price."

"No," Ysoly said. "That is my point."

He wrote while she was still speaking, which he had done to her for nineteen years, and which meant that a sentence was over the moment he had it and the rest of your saying it was noise you were making at a man's hat.

"You are entitled to think it, madam. There is not another line on this quay that would carry two hundred and twelve blocks with eleven hands. Two rim-cities, eight hundred miles of nothing between them, and nine wagons of yours a thread in it." He turned a page. "Passengers this season?"

"One."

The pen moved before she had finished the word.

"One," Vask said. "Well."

"A Warden-surveyor. He paid at the licence office and I am told he asked for me by name, which I do not like and cannot afford to mind."

"They travel." He was still writing. "Are you well fixed for the last quarter's freight?"

"It settles at the far rim. It settles at the far rim every year and every year you ask me on the quay."

"I ask because the paper on your wagons is held two doors from my counting-house and I would rather be the man who mentioned it than the man who did not." He shut the book on his thumb and looked at her, and his face was the face of a man doing a kindness, and it was not a lie. "This is your last crossing."

"It is."

"The Assembly is not ungrateful to a master who has never lost one. When you come off at Tallow Reach and come home there will be a sum owing you and it will be paid. I should be sorry if the season were the one where—"

He did not finish it. He never finished them. He opened the book again and made a small mark somewhere near the top of the page, and shook her hand, and went back down the yard past the fourth wagon, where he said something to Pell about the girl's reading that made Pell laugh.

Ysoly stood at the tailgate of wagon one with the ballast face turned up and nothing left to cut on it. Then she went down the line and counted the water butts, fifteen wet and twenty-one dry and stopped, touching each at the hoop, to the end of the ninth wagon.

Then she turned round and counted them again.

## What News Costs

Thecla Bram kept her counting room over the hide market under the second step, out of the smoke, and had never once been seen in it after dark with a lamp lit, because a lamp in a window told the quay you were still working and roughly what on.

Pell came up the outside stair at dusk with a spare girth over his shoulder and a needle in his hat, because he had learned young that a man who arrives at a broker's with his hands full is a man who has come about something else.

"Corrow," she said. "You're going out."

"Day after tomorrow, first light. Nine wagons." He sat on the stool that was there for sitting on and laid the girth across his knee and started on the split. "I've brought what I've got."

"How much have you got?"

"Half the quarter."

"Half the quarter," said Thecla, as if he had said a third and she was giving him the benefit of it. She never wrote anything down while a man was in the room, which Pell had always taken for courtesy and had lately begun to take for something else.

"It's a forty-one day crossing, Bram. I get paid at the far rim, I get paid on the way back, and you get the whole quarter at the turn with the interest on the half I'm short. That's better than you'd do lending it to a kiln."

"It is better than I would do lending it to a kiln," she agreed. "It is worse than I would do lending it to a man who is going to be here in the spring. Enna is eleven."

He did not stop stitching. That was the thing to do and he did it. "She is."

"The houses put a child on the paper at fourteen. I did not make that rule and I would not use it on you, and you know that, and you also know I will not be the last person holding this paper if the Line goes over." She turned her hand up. "You are not in trouble with me. You are three years from being in trouble with somebody else."

The needle went through and came back. Under the floor the hide market was shutting: poles coming down, a man shouting a price at a man walking away from him, and the smell of green hide and wet rope coming up through the boards the way it had come up through the boards of every room he had ever sat in in this town.

"I've got something else," Pell said.

"Salt or news."

"News."

"News is cheaper," she said, "which is why I always let you." She sat back. "Go on, then."

"There's a passenger on our bill."

"There are passengers on everybody's bill."

"A Warden-surveyor," Pell said. "Sealed of the College. Four blocks of first grade for one man and his own water, on a nine-wagon line with no cook and no taster, when the joint tile goes out four days behind us with two hundred people and a cook wagon and a taster of its own." He bit the thread. "And the Wardenry's own relay goes south in nine days and would have carried him for nothing."

Thecla Bram said nothing at all for a while.

"He asked for the Tamm Line by name," Pell said. "The master said it herself this morning, in the yard, to Vask, with me sitting on the tailgate eight feet off mending a chain. And Fane is riding out ahead of us, and Fane is Third Rank, and there is not a reason on this quay for a Third Rank to be nursemaiding nine wagons."

"Say the name."

"Rasca. Denet Rasca."

She got up and went to the shelf and stood with her back to him, and when she came back she had a strip of paper the width of two fingers and she was already folding it.

"Take a quarter off the interest," she said. "Not the principal. I do not touch principal and you would not respect me if I did."

"A half off the interest."

"A quarter," said Thecla, "off the interest," in the tone of a woman repeating back a figure she had misheard downward, and that was the end of the negotiation, and both of them knew it had been over before it started.

Pell rolled the girth and stood. He had come up the stair owing money and was going down it owing slightly less, and in between he had said nine sentences about a man who had bought lawful passage on a lawful crossing, which anybody could have had for the asking at the licence office window. He turned it over on the top step and it came out clean, the way it always came out clean, and went down into the hide market to buy his rope off somebody else's father.

Behind him, Thecla Bram lit no lamp. She sat in the dark with the strip of paper and did not write on it yet, because there was a question in it she wanted priced before she let anybody else price it: not what a Warden-surveyor was doing on a nine-wagon line, but why the Assembly's counting-house had been quiet about the centre route for a month, when it was never quiet about anything.

"Northbound crossings make the Ferry on day thirteen," she called down the stair. "If there's a line worth writing by then, put it in a hand coming north."

"There won't be," Pell said, from the bottom.

"No," she agreed. "There never is."

## The Sealed Thing

The husking started in the dark, the way she had been told it would: one, then three or four, then the whole pound at once, forty-one animals coughing the night's salt out of their nostrils in a noise like a wall coming down slowly. Hanei stood in the middle of it and understood that she was going to hear this every morning for forty-one days, and that within a week she would stop hearing it.

Sabra Onn went down the line with a lantern held low, not looking at the beasts but listening, and got to the end, and came back.

"Two gallons the head, half a gallon the hask," she said, to nobody, to the yard. "Eleven and forty-one. Forty-two and a half the day. Six days to water."

Nobody answered her. Nobody ever did. It was not, Hanei worked out much later, a thing said to be heard. It was a thing said so that afterwards no one could claim it had not been said.

Denet Rasca came in at the last of the dark with a yard-boy behind him carrying nothing, which meant everything he had was on him. He was thirty-seven and looked like a man who had been indoors for two summers. Under one arm he had a wooden case, hinged and brass-cornered, the size of a long forearm. Under the other, inside his coat, was a hide-wrapped bundle about the size of a folded coat itself, stitched shut with waxed thread. Hanei saw the corners of it before she saw anything else about him. They were worn pale, the way a strap goes pale where it lies against a body.

Ysoly had the way-bill on the tailgate of the fourth wagon and a lamp standing on it.

"Name."

"Rasca. Denet. I'm sorry — Warden-surveyor, sealed of the College of Levels, and I should say that I have paid at the—"

"You have paid. What's in the case?"

"An instrument. A glass. It doesn't like being under anything."

"And that?"

He looked down at his own arm as though he had been asked about a birthmark. "Papers."

"I carry nothing on my wagons I can't name on the bill," Ysoly said. "That is not the Wardenry and it is not the Assembly, it is me. Everything on those nine wagons belongs to somebody else, and the bill is the only thing standing between me and being told at the far rim that I carried something I didn't."

"Levels. Boring logs. Brine assays, and one folded sheet. Forty-one leaves. I counted them when I stitched it."

"Whose survey?"

"Mine."

"Then open it and let me count forty-one leaves."

The yard did not go quiet, because forty-one hasks do not go quiet for anybody, but the hands nearest the tailgate stopped what they were doing without appearing to.

"I can't." He said it without heat and without much apology, which was worse. "There are two seals on it, the College's and my own, and both are struck once. If it comes open it can never be made to look as though it hadn't. It stops being a sealed survey and starts being paper, and paper is worth what the man holding it says it is worth. I'm sorry. It has to go across shut."

"Then somebody else opens it."

"That is the same thing done by another hand."

Ysoly turned her head. "Warden."

Iseul Fane was already mounted at the head of the yard, waiting to ride out ahead of them the way she had ridden out ahead of trains for twelve years. She did not come closer.

"A College seal is not the Wardenry's to break, and I would not break it if it were. The Second Article says what may pass the bounds and it does not make a man's own papers cargo. Weigh it, enter it as passenger's goods, and be done."

Sabra brought the hand-scale over without being asked, because she weighed things, and Denet set it in the pan himself.

"A pound and thirteen ounces," Sabra said, and gave it back, and turned round to the string. "It isn't water."

"I'll open it," said Pell, from somewhere behind the wheel, and one of the Kaddals laughed, and Pell laughed, and then Pell said nothing at all for a while — and Hanei, who had known him eleven days and had not learned this yet, thought he had simply run out of the joke.

She looked at the bundle herself. She wanted inside it the way she had wanted, at nine years old, to see over the wall of the cistern under the north scarp: not for any reason, only because there was a far side and she had not seen it. She was aware of wanting it, and aware that everybody at the tailgate could see she wanted it, and she made her face do nothing, badly.

Ysoly wrote on the bill. Papers, sealed, one pound thirteen, passenger's own.

Then she pushed the sheet along the tailgate, and Denet — without being invited, and possibly without noticing he was doing it — took the pen out of her hand and worked her water figure down the margin. Eleven twos and forty-one halves. Forty-two and a half. Times ten. Four hundred and twenty-five. He set the pen down and looked at the number slightly too long.

"That's right," he said.

"I know it's right."

"No. I'm sorry. I meant that it's right."

Ysoly took the bill back and looked at the margin of her own way-bill, where a passenger had checked her water, and said nothing about it at all.

## Nine Strokes

The masks came out of the fifth wagon in a sack, one to a head: hide cut to the cheekbone, two slits, and behind each slit a shard of green glass sewn under a fold.

"Off the Lees," Pell said, handing Hanei hers. "Fifteen days out. Lightning makes it, men cut it, you wear it. That is the whole of the plain in one sentence and I said it first."

She was still turning it over when Ysoly said, "Hanei."

"Master."

"Fourth wagon, the locker under the seat, the dry one. It goes in there and it stays in there and your blanket goes on top of it. You don't open it. You don't let anybody open it, and that includes the man it belongs to, and it includes me. If it gets wet you tell me before you have finished telling yourself it's nothing."

"Fourth wagon, the locker under the seat, the dry one. It stays in. My blanket on top. I don't open it, nobody opens it, not him and not you, and if it's wet I come to you first."

Hanei took it in both hands. It weighed nothing. She had expected, obscurely, that it would weigh something, and she stood there with a pound and thirteen ounces of stitched hide in her arms while the drovers walked the teams up and the yoke-chains went on, and she was — she could not help it, she knew exactly how little it meant — the only person on the Tamm Line with a charge of her own that morning.

She got it into the locker and the blanket over it, and then, because there was no one else within earshot and there might not be again for six days:

"At the wells. Do you send people back from the wells?"

"Yes."

"Have you—"

"Ask a different question."

Hanei thought about it. "What do I do at the wells?"

"You water forty-one head in shifts through the night with Sabra Onn standing over you, and you don't sleep, and on the seventh morning you find out." Ysoly went up the line without waiting. "Get on the box."

They went out at first light through the gate at the head of the seventh quay, and the tower rang them out the way it had rung out every wagon that ever left Bell Harrow, once for each. Nine strokes, spaced to the roll of the wheels. Then the bell stopped and the town went back to what it had been doing.

Hanei counted them from the box of the fourth wagon with the packet under her heel. Nine. Behind her in the yard the joint tile stood half loaded, thirty-six wagons and two hundred people, and in four days that bell would go on for the better part of an hour and every soul on the quay would stop and listen to it. Nobody had stopped for nine.

The kiln smoke thinned and then was behind them. The crust came up under the wheels grey-white and ridged into plates the width of a wagon, ticking as the sun got onto it, and it went on doing that, and went on doing that. Hanei pulled the mask down. The world turned the colour of a bottle and kept going and did not have a far side to it anywhere.

At the tail of the string Ysoly walked with the tally-stick in her hands, turning it over, looking for the face.

Heads. Water drawn. Ballast. What was owed, and to whom.

There was no face for it. She put the knife back in her coat without cutting anything, and the crossing went south into the glare with one pound thirteen ounces of sealed hide riding in the locker of the fourth wagon under a girl's blanket, and not one of the eleven people who had walked out of Bell Harrow that morning had put a knife anywhere near the thread.
