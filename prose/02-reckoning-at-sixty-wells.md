# 2. Reckoning at Sixty Wells

> Sixty Wells, day six. The Tamm Line comes in to sixty capped shafts and a slate board with nine numbers chalked on it. Sabra Onn, who was in this field in RR 166, tastes all sixty herself and says thirty-four has turned and fifty-one is sweet, and the board's keeper says it is one old woman's tongue. Denet Rasca settles it in a minute with a College-sealed water-glass — and the field goes on ration, not because the water changed but because the board did. Ysoly buys two hundred and twenty gallons at a block the hundredweight, cuts twenty-two blocks onto the debt face of the tally-stick, and owes the Assembly personally on her last crossing. That night Sabra asks Denet what else he has floated the glass against, and he tells her: the centre. The packet is cut open in Ysoly's cell before five people — forty-one leaves, no fair copy anywhere in the world — and Denet admits he wrote it, that he chose the Tamm Line because eleven is a small number of witnesses, and that Iseul Fane is the only Warden of the Third Rank between the rims. Nobody tells her. The line rolls south at dawn with the packet restitched in Pell Corrow's own waxed thread.

## Sixty Lids

They came in on the sixth evening with the glare going off the crust and the beacons throwing shadows for the first time since morning, and Hanei stood up on the box of the fourth wagon to see the water.

There was no water.

There was a quarter-mile of white ground with sixty stone collars set into it, and on every collar a hide lid held down under a ring of stones, and the lids had gone the colour of old rope and were split at the edges where the sun had had them. Numbers were cut into the collars, deep, and packed black. That was the whole of it.

She had carried a picture of this place since she was nine. The picture had a surface in it, and something moving on the surface.

"It's under," Pell said, going past on foot with a trace-chain over his shoulder. "Everything out here is under. Mask up, we're not in yet."

The station ran along the north edge: mud-brick cells, hide awnings poled out from the wall, a walled pound, and two rooms under a tiled roof with the Wardenry's board nailed beside the door. It smelled of rope and wet stone and, under both, of sulphur — the sour-egg breath that came up out of every shaft in the field and that nobody living here had smelled in years.

Between the post and the pound stood the taster's board. Slate, a man's height, in the wall's shade. Sixty numbers in three columns, nine of them ringed in chalk, and the day's date under the last column in a round careful hand.

Hanei read the nine twice and got them by heart, because they were the only numbers on the plain that had anything to do with drinking. Four. Eleven. Nineteen. Twenty-six. Thirty-four. Forty. Forty-three. Fifty. Fifty-eight.

Sabra Onn did not look at the board at all. She went past it with a tin cup in one hand and a bar under her arm, out into the field, and rolled the stones off the lid of the first collar in the first row.

The board's keeper came out from under the awning while the wagons were still coming up: a narrow man of about fifty with chalk to the second knuckle and a slate-rag through his belt.

"Rusk. I keep the board. Nine sweet this season, and the nine on it now were drawn and tasted at first light. Two blocks the crossing and I taste every barrel under your own eye. One block and you take the chalk."

"Neither," Ysoly said. She was already going down the string, touching the butts at the hoop as she came to them.

"Every line takes one or the other."

"Mine has a water-master."

Rusk looked out into the field, where an old woman was on her knees at collar one with her arm down a hole. "That'll take her till dark. There are fifty-one out there that are no good to anybody."

"She does all sixty," Ysoly said, and went on counting.

Five butts wet and twenty gallons in the sixth. Four hundred and twenty-five gallons had gone out of Bell Harrow on nine wagons; six days had drunk two hundred and fifty-five of them; what stood on the crust at the wells was a hundred and seventy gallons and a great deal of empty hide. She got to the ninth wagon, turned round, and counted them again.

Behind her the pound gate was open and the drovers were bringing the string in eight at a time. Forty-one head, all night, in shifts — rope over the collar, hide bucket down and up, the stone trough filled by hand and emptied by eight animals in about the time it took to fill it again.

"Apprentice," Sabra called, without turning round. "You're on the rope till it's light. Not the bucket."

"The rope till light, not the bucket."

"A bucket is a bucket. A rope is thirty feet of hemp over dressed stone, and if it goes you are not lifting anything out of that hole for the rest of the season." Sabra tipped the cup, spat, wiped the rim on her sleeve. "Six."

She capped six and moved to seven.

By full dark she had done thirty and the lamps were lit along the ring, and by the second shift she was working the far edge of the field with a lantern on the stone beside her: an old woman kneeling in the white with a tin cup, spitting.

At thirty-four she did not move on.

She drew a second cup and held it under the lantern and looked at it, which she had not done once in thirty-three shafts, and then took a mouthful and held it and spat that too, and sat back on her heels with her hands on her knees, and stayed there long enough that Hanei, sixty yards off on the rope, stopped paying out and had to be shouted at.

Then Sabra got up, put the lid on, rolled the stones back onto it herself instead of leaving them, and walked in.

## Thirty-Four and Fifty-One

She finished the field at the ninth hour of the morning and came in with her sleeves wet to the elbow, and stood in front of the board and said, "Thirty-four is turning."

Rusk had the rag in his hand and had been about to take the date off and put a new one up. "Thirty-four was drawn and tasted at first light."

"By you."

"By me. Same as every morning for nineteen years."

"Then you tasted it eleven hours before I did, and it has moved since, and it will move again by the time you put your date up."

Under the awning the hands had stopped working without appearing to. Ysoly came out of the second cell with the licence book under her arm and said nothing, which was her way of joining a thing.

"It is sweet. It is a hard well and always was, and a woman who has drunk butt water for six days will call anything hard—"

"Fifty-one," Sabra said.

"Fifty-one has been brack for eleven years."

"Then eleven years is long enough for a hole to change its mind. It is sweeter than four, than eleven and than thirty-four, and it is not on your board."

Rusk laughed, one note, and looked round for somebody to laugh with, and the awning did not oblige him. "It's a tongue. You've a good one. It is still a tongue, and a field can't be run off tongues."

"There is a board here," Sabra said, "because in the year sixty-six a man chalked nine numbers on it at first light and went to his breakfast."

The Warden of the post came out at that: a heavy man in his forties named Aske, Fifth Rank, in shirtsleeves, carrying the toll-book the way another man carries a hat.

"I will not have that said in my field."

"It was my field first," Sabra said.

"Then the board's sealed," said one of the Kaddals from under the awning, meaning nothing by it, and Iseul Fane turned her head. She had been at the corner of the post since before it started, out of the shade, the way she stood everywhere.

"The board is chalked. There is nothing sealed at this station and nothing stamped. Use the word you want."

That was the whole of what she contributed. It went round again — Rusk's nineteen years against Sabra's thirty-seven, one mouth against another mouth, with sixty stone lids lying out in the glare behind them saying nothing whatever.

"Master," Denet Rasca said.

He had come up from the wagons with the brass-cornered case under his arm, and he said it to Ysoly and not to any of the Wardenry, which Hanei noticed and thought about afterwards.

"I can settle it. I'm sorry — I should say I would rather not, that it takes about a minute, and that I would like the Warden to be the one who asks me. Warden."

"What is it?"

"A water-glass. Sealed of the College of Levels." He set the case on the trough coping and opened it: fleece inside, and out of the fleece a graduated tube eighteen inches long, stoppered and waxed at both ends, half full of something clear that moved slowly when he moved it, a small grey disc of lead wired at the neck. "The brine in it was drawn at the far rim under that mark. It floats against whatever you put it in. Heavier than the reference and it rides high; lighter and it goes down; and where it stops, it stops against a scale a hundred and fifteen years of arguing went into."

"It's a piece of glass," Rusk said.

"It is. The seal is what makes it evidence. Anybody can blow a tube." Denet had his sleeve back. "I want a jar with some depth, and the water drawn in front of you and not fetched to me."

Aske sent his own man down with the post's stoneware jar and Rusk went too, and the whole awning walked out after them and stood round collar thirty-four in the white while the bucket went down and came up.

The glass went into the jar. Denet held it upright with a finger on the stopper and let go.

It went down. It bobbed twice, turned a little on its own axis, and settled, and everybody bent to look at a curve of water in a tube in a stoneware pot in the middle of the largest empty place any of them would ever stand in.

"It has to stop moving," Denet said. "A minute."

Nobody spoke for the minute. It was longer than a minute of anything Hanei had ever waited through.

"Brackish. Not foul — nobody dies of it this week. A good way under the mark I would put a barrel below."

"Read the number," Rusk said, and Denet read the number.

They walked the length of the field to fifty-one and did it again, and the glass rode up.

"Sweet. Sweeter than anything I have drunk since Bell Harrow. Sweeter than four and eleven, I would guess — though I have not floated those, and I am not going to say a thing I have not floated."

Aske took the rag off Rusk's belt himself, wiped thirty-four off the board, chalked fifty-one into the gap, and stood back.

Nine numbers. The same nine as at first light, counted, and not one of them the same nine.

"How long," Sabra said, "have you had that in a box on my wagons?"

"Six days."

"And before six days?"

Denet closed the case and put the two brass catches down, one and then the other, and did not answer her; and Ysoly Tamm, who had said nothing since she came out of the cell, watched him not answer.

Then Aske went inside with the toll-book, and came out ten minutes later, and rationed the field.

## A Block the Hundredweight

The front room had a table, the toll-book, a strongbox with two locks in the floor beneath it, and on a ring in the wall a chain with a ten-pound iron cylinder on the end of it — the same cylinder that hung in every counting-house and depot on the Road.

"The field is on ration from noon," Aske said. "One gallon the head the day, for the days of your leg, at the licence rate. Seventy-seven gallons for eleven heads to the Ferry. Anything over that is bought."

"And the forty-one?" Sabra said.

"Not heads."

Nobody in the room found that remarkable except Hanei, who found it remarkable twice and said nothing both times.

"Read me the article," Ysoly said.

Iseul Fane did not open the waxed sleeve. "'Every licensed crossing shall carry water to the measure of two gallons the head the day, and shall answer for it at the far rim. Every well within the bounds is the Wardenry's to open, to close and to ration.' Third Article, original to the year one."

"Both halves in the same four lines. It tells me what I must carry and it lets him tell me what I may draw, and there is no clause saying the second has to be the larger." Ysoly set the licence book on the table and did not open it. "Now say the part you are not saying."

"Two gallons the head the day is a floor for eleven people. The statute has never had a figure of any kind for a hask, and it will not get one this morning."

A hundred and seventy gallons in her butts. A seven-day leg. Eleven heads at two gallons was a hundred and fifty-four of it, answerable at the far rim against a scale, which left sixteen gallons of sweet water for forty-one animals.

"Sabra."

"Sixteen gallons is a third of a pint the head the day. That is not a ration, that is a rounding."

"They go nine days on brine and cake."

"They pull for eight and go down on the ninth, and that is a hask watered to fill leaving here. There is no rotten crust between here and the Kell worth a bar. I have cut two on that stretch in thirty years and neither is on our beacons."

"Then we buy. Warden. What is it."

"A block the hundredweight."

Pell Corrow stopped mending. He had been stitching a girth in the corner since the article, and the needle stopped and stayed stopped, which Ysoly heard from four feet away without turning round.

"It was a block the four hundredweight on your own board yesterday."

"The field was not on ration yesterday."

"The field is not on ration today either. Nine wells were sweet at first light and nine are sweet now. You have the same water you had. What you have not got is a board you can stand behind."

"That is a fair statement of it." Aske was not a clever man and was not pretending to be. "In four days there is a joint tile behind you. Thirty-six wagons, two hundred people, a taster of their own — and I will have to tell their taster that mine called a well sweet eleven hours before a stranger's glass called it brack. Two crossings. In this field."

"Two crossings in this field," Sabra said, "is how the last one was counted."

"I was seven years old when it happened and I have never once wiped that board without thinking about it. If you want to tell me the order that killed them was the Wardenry's and not the wells, I will tell you half the men at Bell Harrow think so and none of them will say it on a roll. And I will still ration you, because if I do nothing and it turns, the two hundred behind you are on my chalk."

Sabra did not answer that.

"Two hundred and twenty gallons," Ysoly said.

The room took a moment over it.

"Master—"

"On top of the seventy-seven. Four hundred and sixty-seven standing when we roll, and two hundred and ninety-seven and a half is the leg. Four days' grace to the Ferry."

"That is what I asked you for at the weighbridge and did not get."

"It is."

"And it is two thousand two hundred pounds of water at a block the hundredweight. Twenty-two blocks. Twenty-one blocks was the wheelwright and the quarter on the paper, and you said so yourself in the smoke with the weighmaster listening."

"Twenty-one was. This is twenty-two."

"Then I am telling you it is too much water."

Ysoly looked at her a while. "Say it again, out loud, so that you have heard it."

Sabra Onn shut her mouth and did not say it again.

"A passenger with a tube took a chalked board off a wall this morning. He was right about thirty-four before either of you opened your mouths — I watched you spit it and I watched him read it, and if you had not agreed I could not have told anybody which of you to believe. There is nothing at this station I could swear to at a weighbridge, and a master who cannot know buys water. Twenty-two blocks. Weigh it out."

They weighed it out, because water at a Wardenry post is weighed and not measured, and it took the better part of the afternoon, with Rusk made to stand where he could see the beam.

Twenty-two blocks of second-grade salt came off wagons three, five and eight and went into the store behind the post, and the wagons rode a little higher for it, which Pell mentioned to nobody at all. Aske entered it in the toll-book: roll number, date, twenty-two blocks against two hundred and twenty gallons at ration, for the Bell Harrow roll at the season's end.

"That goes north in the autumn."

"It goes on the roll. What the roll does after that is not mine."

"The Assembly's counting-house is two doors from your licence office."

"I know where it is, madam."

She signed. Then she went down the line in the last of the light and turned the tally-stick past heads, past water drawn, past ballast, to the fourth face, and cut twenty-two on it at the tailgate of the first wagon where the hands could watch her do it.

Denet Rasca stood at the corner of the post with the case under his arm and watched her cut it, and did not come closer, and went in.

## The Fourth Shift

The third shift came onto the trough somewhere after midnight, and by then Hanei's hands had stopped being hands and had become two things at the end of her arms that let rope down a hole and pulled it up again.

Fifty-one was the sweet one now, so fifty-one was the one they drew, and fifty-one stood at the far edge of the field where the lantern light off the ring did not reach, and the walk back with a full bucket was a hundred and thirty paces over ridged crust in the dark. Eight animals emptied the trough in the time it took to fill it. Then eight more.

Sabra stood at the trough end counting them off and saying the count aloud, one to eight, every shift, all night.

Denet Rasca came out of the ring at the fourth shift with the case under his arm and a stoneware jar in his other hand, going out into the field. Sabra put her arm across the gap in the pound wall, which was not a large gesture, and he stopped as though it had been a wall.

"Fifty-one," he said. "I want it again cold. Water reads different at noon and I did not say so this morning, and I ought to have."

"You said a great deal this morning."

"I said less than I know."

"Yes," Sabra said. "I asked you a question this morning and you put the catches down on your box."

He stood there holding a jar.

"Twenty-two blocks came off those wagons this afternoon," she said. "That is the wheelwright and the quarter on the paper and a good part of what a woman is owed for thirty years, and it went because a board could not be trusted, and the board could not be trusted because you opened a case. I am not saying you were wrong. I tasted thirty-four before you did and I would have said the same in front of anybody. I am saying your glass cost this line twenty-two blocks in one morning at a well station, and I want to know what else you have floated it against."

"Onn—"

"You have carried it against your body for six days and a stitched hide bag with it, and you would not let the master count the leaves. What else have you floated it against."

He said, "The centre."

The trough was running over. One of the drovers said something about it and she did not hear him.

"There is no water at the centre," Sabra said. "There has never been water at the centre. That is the whole of the reason there is a Road."

"There is water at the centre. It is between nine and fourteen feet under the crust and it is fresh, and I know it because I augered eleven bores over two summers and floated nine of them against that glass, and I'm sorry, and I have known since the spring, and I have not told one living person."

Sabra Onn stood in the dark of a well field with sixty stone collars behind her and did not move for some while.

Twenty-four years old, in this field, on the second of the two southbound crossings, filling from shafts a man had chalked sweet that morning. Nine sweet wells to six. Six to two. Two to none, and past turning back with the Ferry seven days ahead, and the days after that were the days she did not describe to anybody. She had come off at the far rim with something under half the people she went in with, and had walked back to Bell Harrow the next season on somebody else's water.

Day twenty was the centre. She had walked over it thirty-one times with a tally of gallons in her head and brine in the butts and had never once put a bar in the brown ground, because the brown ground is what kills you, and everybody knows it, and everybody is right.

She took the jar out of his hand and set it on the coping.

"Come with me.

## Forty-One Leaves

Ysoly's cell was a mud-brick box in the ring with a bench, a lamp, and a hide across the doorway. She was still up, the licence book open in front of her and nothing written in it.

Sabra put the hide back. "Send for the packet."

"Sabra—"

"Send for the packet."

Ysoly looked at the two of them for the length of a breath, then went to the doorway and called for Hanei; and Hanei came in off the rope with her palms raw, went to the fourth wagon herself and lifted her own blanket off it, and carried it in with both hands the way she had carried it out.

Pell Corrow came in behind her without being asked. There was nowhere on nine wagons to be sent, and Ysoly did not try to send him.

Denet set the packet on the bench under the lamp. Oiled hide the size of a folded coat, waxed thread through it in a running stitch, two grey discs of lead struck flat over the thread at the head, the corners worn pale.

"Once it is cut it can never be made to look as it looks now, and I want that said in front of all of you, because in about a minute somebody here is going to wish it had not been. After tonight this is a bag of paper, worth whatever the man holding it can persuade you it is worth."

"Then don't," Ysoly said.

"I have to."

"You have had six days on my wagons to have to."

"I have had five months," Denet said. "And six days. And this afternoon, and the twenty-two blocks."

He took out a small knife and cut the thread at the head of the stitching, one pull. The lead came away on the thread in his hand, both discs together, and he laid them on the bench beside the lamp where everybody could see them lying loose.

Then he counted the leaves out, saying the number of each as he set it down. They were not clean — field sheets, ruled in pencil and inked over afterwards, foxed at the edges, one with a thumbprint in the corner in something that was not ink. The last was folded in four and larger than the rest.

Forty-one.

"Read me one," Ysoly said.

He took a sheet without choosing it. "'Bore nine. Two hundred and forty paces east of Benchmark One. Auger stopped eleven feet four on wet grey clay. Sample floated at' — the figure is here and it will mean nothing to you — 'against reference. Fresh.'" He put it down. "Nine of the eleven bores came up wet and every wet one read fresh. Two were dry, and I have written down that they were dry, because a man who leaves the dry ones out is not a surveyor."

"Who wrote it?"

"I did."

"Say the rest."

"The Wardenry sent me to re-level the centre routes after the crust moved. Nobody asked me for borings. I put them in because the brown ground gave under my boot, and it has given under everybody's boot for two hundred years, and every one of us wrote rotten crust in a notebook and walked round it. Wet ground out there is brine. It is the first thing a driver is taught, it is correct, and it has been correct every time anybody tested it — which is never, because you do not test the thing that kills you." He straightened the pile with two fingers. "I did not find anything. It was lying in the open. I floated a glass on it and it took about a minute."

Under the mud brick the pound was still working. One to eight. One to eight.

"Where is the other one," Ysoly said.

"There isn't one."

"Colleges keep rolls."

"The rule is that a finding goes on the roll before it goes to the body that paid for it. Four lines — surveyor, ground, date, one line of result. It would have been at Tallow Reach by autumn, and there would be a clerk eight hundred miles from here who could swear to what I found, and I could have stopped carrying it." He was looking at the leaves and not at her. "I drafted it twice. I sent neither. I stitched the bag and struck both seals over the thread. It is the only rule I have ever broken, and I broke it by doing nothing, which is how I do most things."

"So there is one of it. On my wagons."

"Yes. And if a lamp goes over on it there is nothing — no copy, no entry, no clerk's abstract. My memory of my own arithmetic, said out loud in a room, which is evidence nowhere on this plain." He put his hand flat on the bench beside the pile without touching it. "That is what it is worth to the people who will want it. Not what it says. That this is the only place it says it."

Pell Corrow, in the corner, had not stitched anything in some minutes.

Ysoly Tamm looked at the forty-one leaves and thought about a yard of rim-oak under her coat with four faces on it and no second one anywhere in the world, on which was written what every hand on her line was owed and what their families got if they did not come back.

"You asked for me by name," she said. "Four blocks of first grade for one man and his own water, when the Wardenry's own relay would have carried you south for nothing in nine days. Why."

"Because you are eleven people, and I could not think of a smaller number that gets a wagon to the centre."

Nobody said anything to that for a while. Hanei, by the doorway with her raw hands behind her, understood that she had heard a thing said in front of her that would be true for the rest of her life, and that nobody in the room had thought about her being there when it was said.

"One more, and I would rather say it than have somebody find it." Denet did not look up. "'Where such water is found, a Warden of the Third Rank or above, standing upon the ground and speaking before two witnesses, may declare the ground about it a hazard, and set a seal upon it, and hold it against all comers.' Fourth Article. It cannot be done by letter and it cannot be done from the rim. Somebody of that rank has to stand on the ground and say it aloud."

"Fane," said Pell, from the corner. It was the first word he had said since the article that afternoon.

"She is the only one of that rank between the rims while this crossing is out, and the next nearest is three hundred and eighty-five miles behind us. I have spent six days walking the instrument of the Fourth Article towards the one place on the plain it must never stand on. She is asleep in the post about ninety paces from here, and she has been perfectly decent to me for eleven years."

"Then we tell her," Ysoly said.

The cell was very quiet.

"Nobody says anything. Four people in a room and a girl in the doorway, and I say we tell the Warden, and not one of you opens your mouth."

Sabra Onn said, "No."

"Say it properly."

"We do not tell the Warden."

Ysoly looked round all of them, one at a time, taking her time about it. Then she reached over and turned the lamp down, which was the nearest thing to an answer anybody got.

## The Board at First Light

The husking started in the dark, and Sabra Onn walked the pound with the lantern low and let it get to the end before she said anything, and it got to the end, and she came back.

"Forty-one," she said, to nobody.

Pell Corrow had a harness needle in his teeth and the packet across his knee on the tailgate of the fourth wagon. He had waxed his own thread the night before, sitting up, and he did the head of the bag in a running stitch that went nowhere near the old holes, and it did not match, and the new thread was pale against hide that had been carried against a man's body for five months.

"You could go through the same holes," Hanei said.

"You could." Pell did not look up. "A bag mended along its own stitching is a bag somebody wanted put back the way it was. That's worth opening. This is a bag that got sat on." He bit the thread and looked at his own work with no pleasure at all. "Nobody steals a mended thing, girl. That is the entire trade I have."

Hanei put it in the locker under the seat and her blanket on top of it, and it weighed a pound and thirteen ounces, and it was heavier going in than it had been coming out, and she knew perfectly well that that was not true and could not make it stop being true.

Denet Rasca had the two lead discs in his coat pocket. He had picked them up off the bench in the end, after standing over them some time, having failed to work out what a man does with two seals that have been struck once and cannot be struck again.

Out in the field, twenty-two blocks lighter, the wagons rode a hand higher on their axles than they had riding in, and every drover on the line had seen it and none of them had said so to the master.

Ysoly came down the string at the last of the dark with the licence book under her arm.

"Apprentice. You were owed this yesterday and did not get it, and that was mine and not yours." She did not stop walking, so Hanei walked. "You are not going back. You have the pound from here. Forty-one head, the half-gallon, the shifts, the count off the trough. You call it to Sabra Onn at dusk, she calls it to me, and I cut it. If you are ever wrong you come and tell me you were wrong before somebody else tells me you were right."

Hanei repeated it back word for word, got to the end, found she could not make her voice do the last part, and did it again anyway.

"Onn is behind you. Say it to her."

Sabra Onn had watered forty-one head across thirty-one crossings and had never lost a traveller to thirst in any of them, and she stood in the grey light with her arms folded and let a nineteen-year-old say her own numbers back to her. When it was done she said, "Fifty-one. Not four and not eleven. Fifty-one is the only hole in this field that anybody proved," and went up to the head of the string.

Then she said it, the way she said it every dawn, to nobody, to the yard, so that afterwards no one could claim it had not been said.

"Two gallons the head, half a gallon the hask. Eleven and forty-one. Forty-two and a half the day. Seven days to water."

Nobody answered her. Nobody ever did.

By the post wall Rusk was putting up the day's date under the last column in his round careful hand. Nine numbers. Four, eleven, nineteen, twenty-six, forty, forty-three, fifty, fifty-one, fifty-eight.

The station's own people came down with their cans while he was still writing, and went to four, and to eleven, and to nineteen. Two men stood at the head of the row a while looking at fifty-one, the way you look at a stranger who has moved into a house you have walked past all your life, and then went to twenty-six.

Iseul Fane rode out at first light the way she had ridden out ahead of trains for twelve years: north to the beacon, then south along it, the crossing stamp on a thong at her neck and the statute in a waxed sleeve she had not opened in four years and had not needed to open yesterday either. She lifted a hand to Ysoly going past. Ysoly lifted one back.

Fourteen days to the centre. Seven to the Ferry and seven on from it, a Warden of the Third Rank riding half a mile out in front the whole way, and forty-one leaves of paper in a locker under a girl's blanket, stitched shut with a drover's thread.

Ysoly Tamm watched her get small against the glare and then stop being anything at all. Then she turned round and counted the water butts, touching each at the hoop, all the way down to the ninth wagon.

Fifteen wet. Seventeen gallons in the sixteenth. Twenty dry and stopped.

Then she went back to the first wagon and counted them again.
