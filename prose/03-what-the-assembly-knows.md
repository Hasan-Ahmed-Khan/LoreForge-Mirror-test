# 3. What the Assembly Knows

> The Dry Ferry, day thirteen. The Tamm Line comes onto the only rock between the rims with a hundred and sixty-nine gallons standing and seventeen dry days ahead of it, and finds the Assembly's shutter open above the depot: Oreth Vask has ridden two hundred and fifty miles on four hask and no wagon and has been waiting three days. He has known a month, by way of Thecla Bram, who has a correspondence and a debtor. He buys their water down from thirty-one blocks to eleven with one word to the depot, says it is not part of anything he is going to say to them, and means it. Then he asks Denet Rasca how deep, and writes the answer down, and stops writing. The offer is five numbered terms and it would save every person on the Line, and the fifth term names Pell Corrow's daughter. That night eleven people argue it out under an awning tied to mooring rings that once held ships -- six for taking it, three for carrying the surveyor on, two who will not say -- and Sabra Onn, whose whole trade dies if the water is real, says dig it at the Centre in front of the joint tile. Ysoly reads them the Fourth Article instead: dig it and you make water standing within the bounds, half a mile from the only Warden of the Third Rank between the rims. At first light Denet says yes and asks for four lines on the College roll, which is the one thing that cannot be bought, and the sale dies on a clerical point. Vask writes two letters, finishes one sentence, and does not finish the other. The Line rolls south at second light with eight hundred and fifty gallons, eleven blocks cut on the debt face, twenty that have no face at all, and a man in the fourth wagon's shadow that nobody speaks to.

## The Only Rock

The wheels had made one sound for seven days: iron tyres over plates of crust the width of a wagon, a tick and a drop and a tick, until it stopped being a sound and became the shape of the air. On the afternoon of the thirteenth it changed, and Hanei was off the box before she had worked out why.

Stone. The wheels had come up onto stone.

The Dry Ferry stood a foot and a half above the plain and no more — a spine of grey rock two hundred paces long with a jetty cut into the end of it, the only rock between the rims. A hide-roofed depot, a walled pound, the cistern's iron lid bolted flat in the middle of the spine like a coin set into a table, and awnings guyed off mooring rings that had been sunk to hold ships. Eighteen wagons out of Tallow Reach had been lying up on the north side two days, and after thirteen days of eleven people saying necessary things over the noise of wheels, a hundred and fifty people talking at once about nothing came off the rock and hit her in the chest.

"Mask off," Pell said, going past with a trace-chain. "You're in. Don't drink anything anybody gives you free."

The depot man met the string with a slate and a rag, the way Rusk had at the wells, because every man on the Road who sells you something meets you with a slate.

"Water's on the board. A block the two hundredweight."

Sabra Onn stopped where she stood. "It was a block the six the year before last."

"The year before last there was still some of ninety-nine in the hole. There has been nothing in four years and there is four years' less in there." He did not look pleased about it. "You can have all of it you can pay for, and there is no ration on this rock, which is more than the Wardenry will say for you six days back."

Ysoly went down the string with her hand out, touching each butt at the hoop. Five wet. Nineteen and a half gallons in the sixth. Thirty dry and stopped. She got to the ninth wagon, turned round, came back up the line and counted them again, and got the same, and everybody watched her get the same.

"Onn."

"Eight hundred and fifty standing when we roll." Sabra had the figures before she was asked; she always did. "Seventeen days to the Cold Well at forty-two and a half is seven hundred and twenty-two and a half, and three days' grace on it, because I am not walking that leg on nothing. We have a hundred and sixty-nine and a half."

"Buy."

"Six hundred and eighty and a half gallons. Six thousand eight hundred and five pounds." She worked it without moving her lips, which was the only part that took her any time. "At a block the two hundredweight that is thirty and a half, and a depot does not sell halves. Thirty-one blocks."

Twenty-two at Sixty Wells. Thirty-one here. Fifty-three blocks of Assembly ballast off the wagons of a woman who owned no salt at all, answerable at the Tallow Reach weighbridge at the Assembly's own assessed price, by the master personally and not the firm.

"Two hundred and forty-six cakes of glasswort as well," the depot man said. "That price is not arguable."

"No," Ysoly said. "It never is."

Denet Rasca came up on foot and set the case on the cistern coping and had one brass catch up before Sabra spoke.

"Leave it shut."

"I can tell you in a minute what you are buying."

"I know what I am buying. I have drunk out of that hole eleven times." She had the tin cup already; she drew at the trough, tasted, spat, and stood looking at nothing for a second. "Brack. Hard brack, the same as it has always been, and it is what we drink for seventeen days, and there is not one other hole between here and the Cold Well. There is nothing for a number to change."

"It would still be worth—"

"The last time you opened that box it cost this line twenty-two blocks in a morning." She wiped the cup on her sleeve. "I am not saying you were wrong. I am saying keep it shut, because a number I cannot act on is a number that only costs me."

He put the catch back down, slowly, and Hanei saw that he did it slowly and filed it with the other things about him she had no use for yet.

Ysoly stood at the head of the jetty and looked at the depot for a while, because she was not looking at the depot. Above it, on the gable where the rock fell away, there was one shuttered window with the Assembly's mark burned into the sill: a factor's room, kept a hundred and fifty years and occupied for perhaps thirty days of it.

The shutter was open.

"Nine wagons do two hundred and fifty miles in thirteen days," Ysoly said. "A man on four hask with no wagon does it in ten, if he does not care what he arrives like. He left the morning after my bell."

"He is fifty-eight," Sabra said.

"Yes."

He came down the outside stair while they were still standing there, one hand on the rail, because ten days in a saddle at fifty-eight is ten days in a saddle at fifty-eight. His face had gone the colour of a fired tile and was peeling along the cheekbones. He had his book.

"Master Tamm. Your water is bought." He said it before the greeting was cold. "Six hundred and eighty and a half gallons out of the depot's cistern, at eleven blocks, entered against the Line at Bell Harrow at the last assessed price and not against your wagons here. You will be filled tonight."

Sabra Onn said, "Eleven. His board says thirty-one. Eleven blocks is six hundred and eighteen pounds the block, and nobody on this rock has sold water at that price in four years."

"No," Vask agreed, "they have not." He opened the book and wrote something in it while she was still finishing. "It is not part of anything I am going to say to you tonight. I would like that understood at the bottom of the stair, and I know perfectly well that you will not believe it, and that you will be right not to. Come up when you have watered. Bring the surveyor."

## The Factor's Room

The room was hot and low and smelled of the rock: wet stone under a hide roof, lamp-black, and old paper that had never quite dried out in a hundred and fifty years. One table, one chair, a shelf of ledgers with the damp in their spines. And chained through a ring by the door, the way it hung in every counting-house and licence office on the Road, a plain iron cylinder the length of a forearm.

Vask saw Ysoly look at it.

"They sealed the Compact on this rock. Downstairs, in the year forty-seven — two councils and eleven salt houses, out here because it was the only ground between the rims that belonged to neither of them, and they sat down and decided what a pound was." He set the lamp where it would not be in anybody's eyes. "Everything I do rests on a sentence a man wrote on this rock, and the sentence is that a gallon of sweet water weighs ten pounds. Sit down, madam. There is one chair and you will have it."

"I'll stand."

"You will not, because you have been on a box seven days and I have been on a saddle ten, and one of us has to be honest about it first."

She sat. He leaned on the sill instead. Denet stood by the door with the case under his arm and did not put it down.

"I have known for a month," Vask said.

Neither of them said anything.

"I would rather you had the shape of it from me at a table than assembled it at a fire, because what you build at a fire will be uglier than the truth and harder to put down afterwards. Bram writes to me. Thecla Bram, of the hide market. Nineteen years, and I have never once paid her for a letter, which is precisely why they are worth opening." He turned a page without looking at it. "In the spring she wrote that a Warden-surveyor had come in off the centre re-levelling and gone to the licence office window to ask what a passage costs on a small line, and which masters were on their last crossing. That is all it was. Nine words at a public window."

"And you priced it," Ysoly said.

"I read it. Priced is what she does." Vask closed the book on his thumb. "There is one reason a College-sealed man does not take the Wardenry's own relay south for nothing and buys nine wagons instead, and it is that he does not want the Wardenry to know where he is going. I have been thirty-four years in this trade. I did not need a second letter."

"Then you knew before my tile was fired."

"I knew before your tile was fired, and I did not have the courtesy to say so on the quay, and you may hold that against me for the rest of your life." He looked at Denet then, properly, for the first time. "Surveyor. How deep."

Denet did not answer at once.

"You have it in a bag on nine wagons going to Tallow Reach, and there is no roll entry anywhere in the world with your name against it, and you know that better than I do." Vask waited. "There is nothing left in this room to protect. How deep."

"Between nine and fourteen feet. Eleven bores. Nine wet. Every wet one fresh."

Vask had the pen moving before the word nine was out of him — nineteen years of it, the mark going down while the sentence was still being made. Ysoly had watched him do that to her on a quay in the smoke and had never once seen him stop.

He stopped.

The pen stayed where it was on the page, and Oreth Vask looked at the wall for about four breaths, and downstairs the northbound crossing laughed at something.

"Fourteen feet," he said.

"Nine to fourteen. I would not say fourteen alone. It reads better than it is."

"It does not read better than it is. It reads exactly as it is." He put the book on the table and turned it round so it faced them, and it was already written: a page of numbered terms in a small institutional hand, dated at Bell Harrow, drafted before a bell had rung nine times over anybody.

"One. The forty-one leaves, and every note, level and log of them, bought of Denet Rasca as the finder, for four hundred blocks of first grade at Bell Harrow, on demand.

"Two. The Tamm Line's water at this station, and the twenty-two blocks at Sixty Wells, struck.

"Three. The paper on the Tamm Line's wagons taken up by the Assembly and cancelled, and the sum owing to the master on retirement paid in the autumn instead of owed.

"Four. A season's wage to each of the eleven, on the roll at Bell Harrow, for the inconvenience of a passenger.

"Five. Corrow's paper at Bram's — sixty-four blocks and the interest at a quarter — taken up and cancelled."

Ysoly Tamm read the fifth term twice and said nothing whatever about it.

"Four hundred blocks," Denet said.

"Ferrant was paid a hundred and six. The worth of forty miles of crust, at the last assessed price of salt." Vask sat down on the sill. "The last assessed price of salt is assessed by us. I say that out loud because you both know it, and because a man who pretended otherwise at this table would deserve everything that came to him afterwards. The Article says the Wardenry shall pay the finder. It does not say who holds the ruler."

"And if he does not sell."

"Then in a season, or in two years, a Warden of the Third Rank stands on that ground and speaks eleven lines and sets a lead disc on a pin, and the Wardenry pays him what we tell it to pay him, and caps the hole with plain-salt and mortar, and appoints a surveyor when it gets round to it. Ferrant asked twice and died asking." He said it without any weight on it at all. "I am the only person in this transaction offering anybody money."

"What will you do with it?"

"Nothing. I want that plain, because you will imagine I have a use for it and I have not. It goes into a box at Bell Harrow, and the box outlives me, and one day a factor who is not yet born opens it and does not know what it was for."

"That is burying it," Denet said.

"Yes."

Denet came off the door frame at last, took the pen out of the inkstand without asking, and worked a figure down the margin of the deed — the way he had worked Ysoly's water figure down the margin of her own way-bill on a tailgate in the dark, because he could not be in a room with a number and a pen and leave them apart.

"Eight thousand nine hundred and sixty gallons," he said. "Four hundred blocks, at the board downstairs. That is what you are offering, said in water." He set the pen down. "I'm sorry. I did that without thinking."

"You did it without thinking," Vask agreed, "and it is the truest thing said in this room tonight."

"I would like the night."

"You have the night. The northbound rolls at first light and anything going to Bell Harrow goes with it, and after that there is nothing north for eleven days." He opened the book again. "That is not pressure, surveyor. That is a crossing hitching up."

## Two Fingers Wide

The pound took forty-one head in shifts of eight off a stone trough forty feet long, filled by hand out of the cistern and emptied by eight animals in about the time it took to fill it again. Hanei had the count. She called it to Sabra Onn at dusk and Sabra called it to Ysoly and Ysoly cut it on the water face, and in thirteen days Hanei had learned that the whole of a caravan is one number handed up a line of three people without being dropped.

Between the second shift and the third she walked out to the end of the jetty to stop being on her feet in one place.

The rock kept the day's heat and gave it back through boot leather long after the sky had gone. Along the north side the Tallow Reach crossing had four fires burning, which was extravagant, and which they could afford to be, being thirteen days from a rim and going the right way.

Pell Corrow was at the third fire with a spare girth across his knee and a harness needle in it.

That was not remarkable; Pell mended things at other people's fires, which was how he got fed twice. What was remarkable was the strip of paper on his thigh under the girth, two fingers wide, and the borrowed pen, and the fact that he was not talking to anybody.

He looked up and saw her and did none of the things a man does when he is caught. He moved the girth six inches so she could sit down.

"You're on the trough count," he said. "You're ahead of yourself and you'll pay for it about four in the morning." He took the needle out and put it in his hat. "Go on, then. Ask it."

"What are you writing?"

"That the Assembly's factor at Bell Harrow rode two hundred and fifty miles on four hask and is sitting in a room over that depot with the shutter open." He held the strip up. Eleven or twelve words, and no name at the top. "Bram will take a quarter off the interest for that. It tells her the Assembly is spending money in a place where the Assembly does not spend money, and she will be selling salt forward on it before the ink is dry at her end."

"You sold us."

"I sold that a surveyor came in off the centre routes in the spring and asked at the licence office window what a passage costs on a small line." He was not defending it. He was reciting it, the way a man recites a thing he has been over on his own many times. "That was a public window. Any clerk in that office could have said it to anybody who bought him a drink, and three of them probably did. I did not know what it meant. I have thought about it every night for six days and I still cannot make myself say I did."

"Bram knew what it meant."

"Bram knew what it meant," Pell agreed. "That is the whole of what you buy when you borrow off her instead of off a house — she is cleverer than you and does not charge you extra for it."

Down in the pound the third shift went onto the trough and Sabra started counting them off aloud, one to eight, the way she did every hour of every night of her life.

"Nine years," Pell said. "Sixty-four blocks and a quarter's interest, and the quarter falls at the turn, and the houses put a child on the paper at fourteen. Enna is eleven." He turned the girth over, found the other split in it, and started on that. "I have paid Bram in coin when I had coin and in news when I had not, and there has been a great deal more news than coin, and every single time it was something anybody could have had for the asking. I am not a man who steals. I am a man who is worth eleven words a quarter."

"You could have told the master."

"Told her what? That I say things? She has known I say things since the year I came on." He bit the thread. "You are waiting for me to look like a man who has done something. I have tried it in my head, girl. It does not come. That is not me telling you I am innocent. It is me telling you what it is like, because you asked, and because by tomorrow night everybody on this rock will be telling you what it was like and none of them were sitting here."

Hanei stood up.

"You'll go to her," Pell said.

"Yes."

"Good. Go before you've thought about it." He did not look up from the girth. "It is worse if you think about it."

Ysoly Tamm was at the tailgate of the first wagon with the licence book shut under her hand, and Hanei told her word for word — the way she had been taught to take an instruction and had never once been taught to give one back. A strip of paper two fingers wide. A borrowed pen. A northbound crossing that rolled at first light. Eleven or twelve words about a shutter, and sixty-four blocks, and a girl of eleven.

Ysoly heard all of it without moving. Then she got up and went down the string with her hand out and counted the butts at the hoop, five wet and nineteen and a half in the sixth and thirty dry and stopped, all the way to the ninth wagon; and came back up the line and counted them again.

"Master—"

"Get everybody," Ysoly said. "The awning by the pound wall. Eleven and the passenger. Now, and not quietly."

## The Count

They came under the awning in the order people come to a thing they have been sent for at night: the two Kaddals first because they had been awake, then Vesh and Ottery, then Rooe and Sennet, then Lyddan doing up his coat, then Bris, then Sabra Onn off the trough with her sleeves wet to the elbow. Hanei stood at the guy-rope end. Pell Corrow came last and sat where he always sat, on the edge of things with something in his hands, and this time there was nothing in his hands and he had to put them on his knees.

Denet Rasca stood outside the awning line, which nobody had asked him to do.

Ysoly set the lamp on the stone and turned it down until it was doing nothing but showing where everybody's face was.

"There is an offer. I am going to read all of it, because if I read some of it you will spend seventeen days deciding what was in the part I did not read."

She read the five terms off the back of the way-bill in the flat voice she kept for figures. Four hundred blocks of first grade for the leaves. The twenty-two at the wells struck, and the water here struck. The paper on the wagons taken up and cancelled, and the master's retirement sum paid instead of owed. A season's wage to each of the eleven, on the roll at Bell Harrow.

"Five. Corrow's paper at Bram's. Sixty-four blocks and the interest at a quarter, taken up and cancelled."

She did not look at Pell when she read it. Everybody else under the awning did.

"The offer is for the packet, and the packet is not mine and it is not yours. It belongs to the man standing outside this awning, and no count taken here moves it an inch. What is mine is nine wagons and a way-bill. So the question I am putting is whether the Tamm Line carries a passenger past this rock, or whether I set him down at a licensed waystation with a Warden relay and a depot and a crossing coming through in four days, and roll south with nothing on my bill but salt." She looked up. "Say it out loud, one at a time, in front of the rest, and then it is said, and nobody gets to have meant something else at the Cold Well. Vesh."

"Set him down. I have driven for three masters and two of them lost their wagons, and I would like once in my life to be paid at a rim by somebody still trading." Vesh had his hat in his hands. "It is not more complicated than that and I am not going to make it sound better."

"Ottery."

"Set him down."

"Rooe."

"Set him down, and say the wage part again in front of everybody, because that is what I am voting for and I would rather own it."

"A season's wage to each of the eleven, for the inconvenience of a passenger," Ysoly said. "Kaddal."

"Set him down."

"The other Kaddal."

"What he said."

"Say your own."

The younger Kaddal looked at the lamp a while. "Then I don't know."

"That is your own. Bris."

"Set him down."

"Lyddan."

"Carry him." Lyddan was twenty-two and in his second season and his voice did the thing voices do. "Nothing after it. Carry him."

"Sennet."

Sennet kept his arms folded. "I would want to know what happens at the Centre. That is not an answer and I know it isn't."

"Apprentice."

"Carry him."

"Thirteen days on this plain, and it costs you nothing."

"I know. That is why I said it fast."

"Onn."

"In a moment," Sabra said. "Let Corrow go first. He has been sitting there four minutes wanting to, and it will poison the rest if it is not said."

Pell Corrow put his hands flat on his knees.

"Take the money. And I will say the rest before somebody else does, because I would rather say it. Bram has had news off me for nine years. She had a line in the spring about a surveyor asking at the licence office window what a passage costs on a small line, and she wrote to Vask, and that is how a man in Bell Harrow knew about a bag none of us had opened. There is a strip of paper on that fire over there going north at first light, and it is mine, and I wrote it two hours ago."

Nobody moved.

"I have not stolen anything from anybody here. That is not me asking you to agree; it is the only sentence I have got, and I have turned it over about four hundred times and it has not broken yet. Sixty-four blocks. Nine years. Enna is eleven, and the houses put a child on the paper at fourteen." He looked up. "Take the money. He gets four hundred blocks, we get a season each, my paper goes, and the master keeps her wagons and goes home to a roof — which is what every one of you has wanted for her since Bell Harrow and none of you will say in front of her."

"That is a good argument," Ysoly said.

"It's the true one."

"It is both. That is what makes it a good one." She did not raise her voice at all. "Onn."

Sabra Onn stood with her wet sleeves and the cup still in her hand.

"Two gallons the head," she said. "Half a gallon the hask. Eleven and forty-one."

Pell shut his eyes.

"He is still eleven," Sabra said, to the awning generally. "He drinks his two gallons and waters his string and ropes his wagon at dawn like the rest of us, and anybody with a different idea about that can take it up with me at the trough. I have seventeen days of brack ahead of me and forty-one head to get to the Cold Well, and I am sixty-one years old."

## Day Twenty

She put the cup down on the stone.

"Carry him. And when we come onto the brown ground on the twentieth day, put a bar in it."

Vesh said, "Onn—"

"Day twenty. I have walked over that ground thirty-one times with brine in the butts, and I went round the soft patches, because the soft patches are what kill you, and I was right every time." Her voice did not change at any point. "In the year sixty-six I filled at Sixty Wells off a board a man had chalked at first light. My crossing went south past this rock with what it had, and on the twentieth day out we buried four people. I have thought about the twentieth day every year since, and until six days ago I thought I was thinking about thirst.

"If that bag is right, they went down on top of fourteen feet of sweet water with a spade in the wagon. They did not die of the plain. They died of nobody being able to read the ground.

"So. Paper can be bought, and it can be burned, and a man's seal can be taken off him eight hundred miles away by somebody who never leaves a counting-house. Nobody has yet worked out how to buy a hole with water standing in it. The joint tile is four days behind us the whole way — thirty-six wagons, two hundred people, a taster of their own — and it comes through the Centre on the twenty-fourth day. Dig it before they get there, and let two hundred people walk past it and look down."

Nobody said anything for some while. The trough ran, and the third shift drank.

"Rasca," Ysoly said. "Inside the awning. Read her the Fourth Article. The first sentence, not your favourite part."

Denet did not have to open anything. "'Water rising or standing within the bounds is a hazard to the crossings and is held in trust by the Wardenry.'"

"Again."

"'Water rising or standing within the bounds.'"

"There is an argument," Ysoly said to the awning, "which the surveyor made in my cell at Sixty Wells and which I have carried seven days like a stone in a boot: that a lens nine feet under the crust is not water rising and is not water standing, that the Article does not reach it, and that a rim advocate could stand up and say so and not be laughed at." She let that sit. "Now put a bar in it. Dig your hole and let it fill, and by evening there is water standing inside the bounds of the Meniscus with a Warden of the Third Rank riding half a mile up the beacon line — and not one advocate at either rim could argue anything for you at all, because you will have built the thing the Article was written about with your own hands."

"They will seal it anyway," Sabra said.

"They may. It is a different word when we hand it to them."

"Then we hand it to two hundred people first."

"With Iseul Fane in front of us the whole way." Ysoly turned the lamp up a notch, so that people had to see each other properly. "That is the whole argument, and it has no floor under it, and I have been walking round the outside of it since the wells.

"Here is what I have got. The way-bill says Bell Harrow to the far rim, one man, his own water, no freight above what he carries. I wrote that, and I signed it under a Warden's hand with the tiles cooling on the rack behind me, and the bill is the only thing standing between me and being told at a weighbridge that I carried something I did not. I am not going to be a master who set a paying passenger down on a rock because a factor made her a good offer at the right hour of the night. He rides. That is not a count, it is not a kindness, and nobody has to like it."

Ottery said, "Master, I've changed. Carry him."

"Why."

Ottery looked across the awning at Pell Corrow, and then at his own boots. "Because of what was read out fifth."

"That is not a reason."

"No," said Ottery. "It isn't."

"Bris?"

"Same."

"Then it is four to set him down, five to carry him and two who will not say; an hour ago it was six and three; and at the Cold Well it will be something else again, and none of it has decided anything. What it has done is put every one of you on the record in front of ten other people, in your own voice. That does not come off." Ysoly stood up. "The Centre is seven days. Nobody puts a bar in that ground, nobody says the word water in front of the Warden, nobody writes to anybody, until I say it at the Centre with all of you standing there. Say it back."

"Nobody puts a bar in the ground until you say it at the Centre," Hanei said, word for word, before she could stop herself.

And then the rest of them said it, raggedly and at different speeds, eleven people under a hide awning at two in the morning saying one sentence back to a woman with a lamp — and Pell Corrow said it too, and nobody looked at him while he did.

## The Letter South

The northbound crossing was hitching at first light, eighteen wagons and a great deal of shouting, and the noise of it came in at the depot door while Denet Rasca stood at the table and said yes.

"Yes. Four hundred blocks. I will sign it."

Vask had the pen already inked.

"One condition, and I'm sorry — I should say it is not a haggle. I would take three hundred, and two, and I would take the water off this line's account and nothing else at all." Denet had his hands behind his back like a man being examined. "The finding goes on the College roll. Four lines. Surveyor, ground, date, one line of result. And the sale entered beside it in your name, so that anybody at the far rim in a hundred years can see that the Assembly bought it and what it cost."

The pen did not move.

"No," Vask said.

"It is four lines in a book eight hundred miles from anywhere."

"It is a book that is read." He set the pen down across the inkstand, squarely, the way he did everything. "You are asking me to buy a thing and then let it be announced. Forward salt does not care who owns the water, surveyor. It does not care whether it is capped or dug or held in trust. It cares that there is water. Four hundred blocks buys silence. It does not buy an entry."

"Then I can't."

"You are throwing away four hundred blocks and this woman's roof for a clerical formality."

"Yes," Denet said. "I'm sorry — I should say that I would like to sign it, that I have wanted to since the stair last night, and that I am not doing this because I am brave. It is the rule I have already broken once, and I broke it by doing nothing, and doing nothing is available to me again this morning and I can feel it standing there. If the number does not go on the roll, it never happened. That is not a formality, factor. That is the only thing a level is."

Ysoly Tamm stood by the door with her licence book under her arm and did not say one word, and it cost her more than anything she had done in thirty years on the salt.

"Very well," Vask said, and drew the north bag and the south bag across the table.

He wrote the first letter in about four minutes, and did not hurry any of it, and folded it and sealed it and held it up.

"North, with the crossing outside, to my own counting-house at Bell Harrow. It concerns the paper held on your wagons, which falls due at the turn, and which is held two doors from my office by people who do as I ask because I have asked them for thirty-four years." He put it in the north bag. "I would rather be the man who mentioned it than the man who did not."

He took a second sheet.

"South, in the depot's bag, to the keeper of the roll at the Level House at Tallow Reach. It says that Denet Rasca, sealed of the College of Levels, ran a survey of the plain's centre in the seasons of two hundred and one to two hundred and three, sealed his findings in the spring of this year, entered nothing on the roll, and has carried the packet on his person since. Six lines, and not one thing in them that is untrue, which I am glad of, because I have never been good at the other."

Denet did not move.

"Your College will lift the seal — its own statutes leave it no choice, and you know them better than I do. And forty-one leaves in the hand of a man who is not a sealed surveyor are not a survey. They are a story about water, told by somebody with a reason to tell it. I will not have to buy them, or burn them, or take them off you, and the Article could not have taken them either." He sealed it. "The south bag goes on the joint tile in four days. It will be at the Level House four days after you are. I am telling you that because you would rather know, and because a man who does not tell you is hoping you will hurry and make a mistake."

He put it in the south bag and closed the flap.

"Warden Fane sleeps at the relay eighty paces from this table," he said. "She is of the Third Rank, she is the only one between the rims while you are out, she has been told nothing at all by anybody, and the reason for that is—"

He stopped, and opened the book, and made a small mark somewhere near the top of the page.

"Well," he said.

He shook Ysoly's hand at the depot door, because he had shaken her hand on a quay in the smoke for nineteen years, and it was not a lie then either.

Iseul Fane rode past the head of the string at first light the way she had ridden past the head of a string for twelve years, and then stopped, which she did not do.

"Master. There is a factor of the Salt Assembly on this rock who was standing in a yard at Bell Harrow the morning I stamped your tile. He came out on four hask with no wagon, at fifty-eight, and he has been in that room three days waiting for nine wagons." Iseul did not look at the depot while she said it. "I wrote your endorsement myself — one man, his own water, no freight above what he carries — and I have thought about that line for thirteen days. Is there anything on your way-bill that is not on your way-bill?"

"No," Ysoly said.

Iseul Fane sat her animal a moment longer. "Two days to the Lees. The Wardenry will have your stage endorsed there."

They loaded through the morning and rolled at second light, which is what a crossing does when it has been up all night and cannot afford the day.

Ysoly went down the string with her hand out. Twenty-eight butts full. Ten gallons in the twenty-ninth. Seven dry and stopped — eight hundred and fifty gallons of hard brack out of a rock cistern, two hundred and forty-six cakes of pressed glasswort on top of it, and the wagons sat down on their axles like animals lying down. More water than the Tamm Line had ever rolled out of anywhere in thirty years.

She got to the ninth wagon, turned round, and counted them again.

Then she took the tally out from under her coat and turned it to the debt face at the tailgate of the first wagon, where the hands could watch her do it, and cut eleven.

"Twenty," Sabra Onn said.

"There is no face for twenty."

"No," Sabra agreed.

Ysoly put the knife back in her coat.

Sabra walked to the head of the string and said it to nobody, to the rock, the way she said it every dawn so that afterwards no one could claim it had not been said.

"Two gallons the head, half a gallon the hask. Eleven and forty-one. Forty-two and a half the day. Seventeen days to water."

Nobody answered her. Nobody ever did.

Pell Corrow came up the line last with a needle in his hat and a girth over his shoulder, got onto the box of the fourth wagon, and said something about the near-side leader on eight; and Bris answered him about the leader on eight and about nothing else, and that was how it was going to be.

Hanei put her blanket over a pound and thirteen ounces of stitched hide in the locker under the seat, and got up beside him.

The stone let go of the wheels and the crust took them back, tick and drop and tick, and by the second beacon the Dry Ferry was a grey line with a hide roof on it — and behind them the shutter above the depot stood open the way it had stood open for three days, and a man who had never in his life walked further than the length of a quay sat behind it with a book and four days to wait.
