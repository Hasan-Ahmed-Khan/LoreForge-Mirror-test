# 4. The Centre

> The Centre, day twenty. The crust goes brown and soft and forty-one hask stop dead in the traces, because two centuries have taught them that wet ground on the plain is the thing that kills you, and they are right about everything except this. Iseul Fane is at Benchmark One before them, having counted eleven stakes and read the numbers cut into them, and she asks Ysoly a second time what is on her way-bill and is not answered. Denet Rasca reopens bore nine, two hundred and forty paces east of the benchmark, and the College glass reads fresh in front of eleven witnesses and a Warden of the Third Rank; Hanei drinks and laughs before she can stop herself. Then the reversal: Denet has spent five months checking his own arithmetic hoping to find the mistake that would make it eighty feet, because eighty feet is a shaft, a windlass crew and a padlock, and a padlock has an owner who can be argued with. Nine feet is a spade and an afternoon and belongs to nobody, which means it cannot be sold, which means the four hundred blocks were never for the water. Ysoly works out why Oreth Vask stopped writing at the Dry Ferry and why his letter went north about her wagons; Pell Corrow goes quiet, does the sum, and says out loud that a debt written in blocks of salt is the one thing on the plain the water makes cheaper. Sennet reaches for the strict reading of the First Article and Denet closes it with his own auger logs -- salt to thirty-one inches at the thinnest, over the arm by any reckoning of the arm -- so the site is inside the bounds in the finder's own hand. Iseul recites the whole Fourth Article from memory, correctly, and then opens the waxed sleeve and reads it again, which she has not needed to do in four years. Ysoly rules: dig it, here, in front of the Warden, before the joint tile comes through on the twenty-fourth with two hundred people and spades of its own -- and dumps a hundred blocks of Assembly ballast onto the brown ground to buy back the day it costs, owed personally at the far rim. The hole is nine feet by the third hour of the morning and has two feet of water standing in it by the time the lamp burns down. At dawn Sabra Onn walks the pound and counts forty coughs where there were forty-one, drinks the first sweet water of her life that nobody carried, pours a full cup out on the ground on purpose, and then says the ration figure unchanged.

## Benchmark One

The change came under the wheels before it came into the eye.

Twenty days of the one sound — iron tyres over plates of crust the width of a wagon, tick and drop and tick — and on the forenoon of the twentieth it went out of the train the way a fever goes out of a room. No tick. No drop. The wheels ran on something that did not answer them at all.

Hanei pushed her mask up onto her forehead, which nobody does at noon, and looked at the ground with her own eyes.

Brown. Not the scorched brown of the Lees five days back, where the lightning-glass lay in green-black sheets and the glare came up at you as well as down. This was flat and dirty and even, brown to the place where the world stopped, like the ash under a kiln that has been raked and left. The wheels were putting two lines in it, and the lines were not closing up behind them.

She got down and set a boot in it. It gave. It took the boot and kept it — a print with the nail-heads legible in it, standing there in the ground, when nothing between here and Bell Harrow had held a mark of any kind.

Then the string stopped.

It stopped the way a hask stops, which is not the way anything else stops. The near-side leader on the second wagon put its splayed foot down, felt the pad spread on something soft, and set itself: weight back, head down, fourteen hundred pounds become a thing at the end of a chain that was not going to move. The three behind it set themselves. Inside a hundred paces the whole forty-one were refusing in the traces, and the wagons walked up on them and had to be braked.

"Leave them." Pell Corrow was already off the box and going down the line at a trot. "Don't tap them. Nobody taps anything. They're right."

"They're right?" Hanei said.

"By everything anybody ever taught them." He had a flat hand on the near shoulder of the leader on eight and was leaning on it, not pushing. "Soft ground under the pad is rotten crust, and rotten crust is brine, and brine is how you lose a wagon and a string and a season. That animal has been told so by its mother and by four generations of men with sticks. It is the best thing about it." He clicked his tongue and got nothing. "Sennet. Bring them across on foot, one at a time, hand on the shoulder."

It took two hours. Forty-one animals were walked over the brown ground by eleven people who had been taught the same thing and had been right about it their whole lives, and they went the way something goes when it is doing what it is told and not what it knows.

Ysoly Tamm walked at the head of it and did not look out at the plain. Hanei had noticed that in Bell Harrow and had gone on noticing it for twenty days: the master looked at wheels, at hide, at hands, at the beam of a scale, and never once out. Here she looked down.

The cairn stood a quarter-mile on, and it was the only thing standing anywhere.

Grey stone, chest high, dry-laid, with a bronze plug set flat in the top of it and worn bright by two centuries of people putting a thumb on it. Benchmark One. Every level on the Meniscus was worked back to that plug; every beacon between the rims sat at so many feet above or below a disc of metal in a heap of stones at the dead middle of nothing.

Past it, running east, there were stakes. Hanei counted them from the box because counting was the whole of what she had learned to do: eleven, spaced out into the glare, each with a ring of spoil heaped at its foot. And past the stakes, four hide tents that had stood through two summers and gone the colour of old rope and split along the ridge, and under a frame of lashed poles a hand-auger rig with the grease gone black and hard in its collar, and a tally board leaning against a tent peg with nothing chalked on it any more.

The Warden's animal was picketed at the cairn.

Iseul Fane came in from the stake line on foot, and she had her mask off and her sleeves down and she was carrying nothing, and she stopped at the head of the string in the place a Warden stops.

"Master."

"Warden."

"Eleven," Iseul said. "Eleven stakes, every one numbered and cut, and nine of them with a second mark I do not know the meaning of. Eleven spoil rings. The salt in them is grey-white and coarse and lies on grey clay, and under the grey clay in nine of them there is a wet ring that smells of clay and does not smell of salt." She said it in the flat order of a thing being entered on a page. "I came in at the third hour and I have walked the line twice."

Ysoly said nothing.

"At the Dry Ferry I asked you whether there was anything on your way-bill that was not on your way-bill, and you told me there was not." Iseul did not raise her voice; she had never once raised it in thirteen days. "I am going to ask you again, here, and I would like you to take your time over it."

The husking had started somewhere down the string, one animal clearing its nose, and stopped again.

Ysoly Tamm stood with the licence book under her arm and looked at a woman she had been lifting a hand to across half a mile of glare since Bell Harrow, and did not answer her.

Iseul waited exactly as long as a person waits.

"The Wardenry will want your stage endorsed," she said. "Bring the tile to the cairn before dark."

## Bore Nine

Bore nine stood two hundred and forty paces east of the benchmark, and Denet Rasca walked it out in paces rather than looking for the stake, because he had walked it out in paces eleven times in two summers and his legs knew the number better than his eyes did.

Hanei came behind him with the packet in both hands. It had come out of the locker under her blanket for the second time in fourteen days and it still weighed a pound and thirteen ounces, and Pell's pale thread was still crooked across the head of it where he had sewn it nowhere near the old holes.

"Nine," Denet said, and put his hand on the stake.

He got the folded leaf out — not the map, a field sheet, ruled in pencil and inked over afterwards — and read it to himself and then aloud, because Ysoly was standing behind him and Ysoly did not like a man reading to himself.

"'Bore nine. Two hundred and forty paces east of Benchmark One. Auger stopped eleven feet four on wet grey clay.'" He put it back. "I would rather do it again than show anybody a sheet. A sheet is my handwriting."

The rig came apart into four pieces and went together into four pieces, and the grease in the collar had to be cut out with a knife and worked soft between the hands before the barrel would turn. Eleven people and a Warden of the Third Rank stood round a hole in the ground and watched a man do a job he had always done alone.

At four feet the spoil coming up was salt: grey-white, coarse, ringing faintly when it went off the blade onto the heap.

At six it was grey clay, heavy and dead, and the barrel turned harder.

At nine feet four the crank went light and then wet, and Denet stopped and stood back and let Lyddan draw it, and what came up on the auger head was clay the colour of a bruise with water standing in the flights of it.

Nobody said anything.

"It has to be drawn and not fetched," Denet said. "I'm sorry — I should say that I would not accept it myself if somebody handed me a full jar and told me where it came from, and neither should any of you. Warden, will you send the bucket down."

Iseul Fane looked at him for a moment. Then she took the hide bucket off the rig frame herself, and paid the line out through her own hands, and drew it, and set it on the ground in front of eleven people, because the one thing nobody in this trade would ever accuse a Warden of was lifting the wrong bucket.

The case had nowhere to go. There was no coping here, no trough, no cut stone, nothing on the whole of the Meniscus at this spot that was harder than the ground; and Denet stood holding a brass-cornered box with a glass in it that had been dropped once in his life, and looked for somewhere to set it down, and in the end Sabra Onn put both hands out and he laid it across them.

He took the tube out of the fleece. Eighteen inches, stoppered and waxed at both ends, a small grey disc of lead wired at the neck, and the reference brine inside it moving slowly when he moved it — drawn and sealed at the far rim under the College's mark, and the only one of its kind between here and there.

The stoneware jar went into the wet clay to fill. The glass went into the jar.

He held it upright with a finger on the stopper and let go.

It rode up.

"It has to stop moving," Denet said, and no one had asked him to say it. "A minute."

The minute went by in a silence with forty-one animals in it, which is not a silence, and Hanei found she was counting the husks the way she counted them at dusk and could not make herself stop.

"Fresh." He was bent over with his hands on his knees, reading a curve of water in a tube in a pot in the middle of the largest empty place any of them would ever stand in. "Read the number, somebody, out loud, so that it is not only me saying it."

Iseul read the number.

"Fresher than fifty-one," Denet said. "Fresher than anything at the Ferry, fresher than anything I have drunk since the far rim. That is the third time I have floated that glass in this hole and it is the third time it has said the same thing, and this time there are twelve of you watching it."

Sabra Onn drew a cup out of the bucket without asking anybody, and tasted, and did not spit.

She stood there with the cup in her hand for a while.

"Give it here," Hanei said.

"Apprentice—"

"Give it here."

She got it, and drank the whole of it, and it was cold from nine feet of clay and tasted of wet stone and nothing else — no sulphur, no iron, no salt riding at the back of the throat, none of the flat hard brack off a rock cistern, none of the hot leather of a butt that had come eight hundred miles.

Hanei laughed.

It was out of her before she knew it was coming, one flat honest note of it in the middle of eleven people standing round a hole, and she got a hand over her mouth about a second too late and looked at the ground and went scarlet under the salt-burn.

"Don't," Ysoly said.

"Master, I'm—"

"Don't apologise for it. Nobody here is going to." Ysoly Tamm was looking at the bucket. "Rasca. If a man wanted that out of the ground and did not have an auger, what would it take him?"

Denet straightened up with the glass in his hands.

"A spade," he said. "And an afternoon."

## Eighty Feet

They lay up through the worst of the day the way a crossing always does, under hide pulled off the wagons and guyed to the wheels, and for the first hour nobody said one word about it.

Then Denet Rasca sat down on a spoil ring with a boring log across his knee and a pencil, and worked his own figures down the margin.

He did it the way he had done it on the tailgate of the fourth wagon in the dark at Bell Harrow and on the margin of Vask's own deed at the Ferry, because he could not be in the same place as a number and a pencil and leave them apart. Eleven depths in a column. Nine of them with a mark. He added the nine and divided and wrote the mean at the foot and sat looking at it.

"Say it," Ysoly said.

"Ten feet eight." He rubbed it out with his thumb and did it again, and got the same, and did not rub it out the second time. "I have done that about forty times since the spring. I'm sorry — I should say what I was doing, because it is not honest to do it in front of you and not say it. I have been looking for the mistake that makes it eighty."

"Why eighty."

"Because at eighty feet all of you keep your lives."

Under the hide somebody shifted and stopped shifting.

"Eighty feet is a shaft." He put the log down. "Not a hole. A shaft. The crust will not stand on its own, so the first twenty feet has to be dressed stone, and there is no stone on this plain but the Ferry, seven days north. It is a windlass house and a crew of six and a season and a great deal of somebody's money, and when it is finished it is a thing somebody owns, because somebody paid for it. Go and look at the Cold Well. Sixty feet, dressed for twenty, six men on the windlass — and a Warden of the Fifth Rank on top of it with a toll-book and a strongbox and a padlock. That padlock is the government of the southern leg, and every soul out here has arranged their whole life round it and nobody thinks it strange."

"And nine feet," Sabra said.

"Nine feet has no owner. There is nothing to own. There is a spade in wagon seven and there is an afternoon." Denet had his hands flat on his knees. "The first man who digs it has water standing in a hole by evening. Then somebody digs another twenty paces off, and there is nothing — not in the statute, not in the Compact, not in any counting-house at either rim — that reaches the second hole. Deep water can be held, and held water can be sold and tolled and licensed and sued over and argued about at a table by people who are good at tables. That is a fight, and it would go on thirty years, and some of you would win parts of it." He looked up. "This is an afternoon. And then it is over, and every institution on this plain is a thing that used to be necessary."

Ysoly Tamm had not moved through any of it.

"He stopped writing," she said.

"Master?"

"Vask." She was looking out from under the hide at eleven stakes in the brown ground. "Nineteen years that man has written down what I was saying while I was still saying it. He asked you how deep, and the pen was already going, and you said nine to fourteen, and he stopped." She turned the licence book over in her hands without opening it. "I thought he was appalled. He was working out that there was nothing to buy. Four hundred blocks was never for the water — nobody buys water a man with a spade can have. It was for the quiet, and the quiet at nine feet lasts as long as it takes one person to walk out here and think about it.

"And then he wrote north. Not about the packet. About the paper on my wagons, which falls due at the turn, held two doors from his office by people who do what he asks." She put the book down. "A man buying something does not write that letter. That is the letter of a man getting his money out."

Under the hide, Pell Corrow had gone quiet.

Nobody but Hanei noticed at first, because Pell going quiet is not a thing that announces itself; it is only that the running commentary stops, and the hands keep working, and after a while you find that a man who has not stopped talking since Bell Harrow has said nothing for four minutes.

"Sixty-four blocks," he said.

"Corrow," said Sabra.

"No. Let me get it out, because if I sit on it I will be sitting on it at the Cold Well." He had a girth across his knee and had not put a stitch in it. "My paper at Bram's is not written in coin. Nobody's is. It is written in blocks — sixty-four of second grade and the interest at a quarter, nine years at it and another nine to come. If a block goes to a tenth of what it was in the spring, sixty-four blocks is a season's wage and a bit, and I clear it, and Enna does not go on that paper at fourteen. She does not go on it at all."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"And Bram is finished," Sabra said. "She has warehouses."

"Yes."

"And every house on that quay, and every man on this line, and the master's roof, and mine."

"Yes," Pell said. "I know what I have just said and I have heard it, and I am not going to pretend I did not think it." He picked the needle up. "There it is. I am the only one under this hide that it is good for, and I would rather you all heard it off me than worked it out about me at the Cold Well."

Ysoly Tamm got up and went out from under the hide into the glare, and went down the string with her hand out, touching every butt at the hoop as she came to it.

Eighteen wet. Twelve and a half gallons in the nineteenth. Seventeen dry and stopped.

She got to the ninth wagon and turned round and came back up the line and counted them again, and got the same, and a hundred and forty paces away there was a stake with a hide bucket standing at the foot of it and wet clay round the rim, and she counted them twice anyway.

## Salt to the Arm

The heat came off it at four, and Sennet said the thing everybody had been holding.

"It isn't salt."

"Say the rest of it," Ysoly said.

"The First Article says the bounds are ground below the old shoreline and bearing salt to the depth of a man's arm. I have heard that read out at a weighbridge twice." Sennet had his arms folded, which was how he said everything. "This is brown and it takes a bootprint and it gives like ash. It is not the crust. If it is not within the bounds, the Fourth Article does not reach it — not the seal, not the hold, none of it — and the Article says what the Wardenry answers for beyond the bounds, which is nothing."

"That can be argued," Denet said.

"Then argue it."

"It can be argued in good faith by a rim advocate who has never been out here, and no court at either rim has ever ruled on the bounds, and it would take about four years." He was already moving. "And it is wrong. I know it is wrong because I am the man who proved it wrong, before I knew what I was proving."

He knelt at the nearest spoil ring and dug his hand into it and came up with a fistful.

"The brown goes down about a hand. It is clay worked up into the salt by the wet underneath, and that is why it gives. Under the brown it is this." He held it out — grey-white, coarse, and it ran off his palm the way it ran off a blade at the kilns. "Salt, and it goes on being salt past the elbow, and the grey clay does not begin until below the arm. Eleven bores, eleven spoil rings, still heaped where I left them two summers ago, and any of you can kneel down and rub one between finger and thumb without taking my word for anything. The thinnest of the eleven ran salt to thirty-one inches." He let it fall. "That is over the arm by any reckoning of the arm anybody has ever made. This ground is inside the bounds, in my own hand, and it is the only piece of work I have ever done that I wish I had done badly."

Iseul Fane had come up while he was on his knees and had not interrupted him, which was itself a kind of statement.

"That is a correct reading of the First Article," she said.

"I know."

"Warden," said Lyddan, who was twenty-two and in his second season, "when they seal us—"

"Stamp." It came out of her without a break in it, the way it had come out of her at a licence office window in Bell Harrow twenty days ago. "A stamp is permission and a seal is a hold. Your master is stamped. Nothing on this crossing has been—"

She stopped.

It went round the whole of them in the time it takes to draw a breath.

"The Fourth Article," Iseul said, "was sealed in the year one hundred and sixty-seven, after the Brackening. It is the only amendment the statute has ever had."

Then she recited it, standing on brown ground with her hands at her sides: water rising or standing within the bounds is a hazard and is held in trust by the Wardenry; a Warden of the Third Rank or above, upon the ground, speaking before two witnesses; forty days to the roll at Bell Harrow or it is no seal from the day it was set; the seal to lie upon the ground and the water and upon nothing else, not upon survey, level, note, log nor instrument, nor upon the person carrying the same; the finder to be paid the worth of the ground at the last assessed price of salt.

And then the last of them.

"'Provided lastly, that where the master of a lawful crossing stands upon sealed ground, holding his tile whole, he may require of the Warden, before the same two witnesses, that the survey be begun before the turn of the season next following; and the Warden shall enter the requirement upon the roll beside the seal. A requirement so made is not undone by the after-breaking of the tile. But a tile broken before the speaking of it is no licence, and he that holds no licence may require nothing. And if the season turn and no survey be begun, the seal is lifted of itself, and the ground is free.'"

Nobody moved.

Then Iseul Fane put her hand inside her coat and took out the waxed sleeve, and opened it, and read the Fourth Article again to herself with her lips not moving, and put it away.

She had carried that sleeve for four years without needing it, and every hand on the Tamm Line had seen her carry it, and every one of them watched her open it.

"I have got it right," she said. "I wanted to be certain."

"Are you certain of the rest?" Ysoly said.

"No." Iseul looked out at eleven stakes. "The Article says where such water is found. It does not say by whom, and it does not say when, and it has never once had to. What I have found at this cairn is eleven holes a man augered two summers ago and a bucket of wet clay I drew with my own hands." She said it evenly, and it cost her something to say it evenly. "I will not speak a sentence on this ground that I cannot square with what is on it."

"Then we'll dig you a hole," Ysoly said.

It went very quiet.

"Master—"

"The joint tile is four days behind and comes through here on the twenty-fourth. Thirty-six wagons, two hundred people, a cook wagon and a taster, and Vask on it or I have never known him. Somebody with two hundred mouths to feed puts a spade in this ground because a drover told him a story at a fire, and there is nobody standing here with a tile at their neck when it happens." Ysoly had the thong out and the fired clay in her fist, and she did not hold it up. "Then it goes the way Ferrant went. Sealed in a morning, capped with plain-salt and mortar, surveyor appointed when somebody gets round to it, and a man asking twice and dying asking.

"So we dig it. Here, in front of the Warden, before they come, with every one of you on the ground and my tile whole at my neck. That is not a count and nobody has to like it."

Sabra Onn said, "It costs a day."

"It costs a day and we have three."

"We have three to the Cold Well and the joint tile is four out. You cannot stand here for four days on three days' grace."

"No," Ysoly agreed. "Vesh. Rooe. A hundred blocks off wagons three, five and eight, laid off square on the ground where the glare will find them, and every one of them counted onto the stick before it comes off the axle."

Nobody argued. Vesh took his hat off and put it back on, which was the whole of what Vesh had to say about it, and went to wagon three.

Ballast came off the Tamm Line for the first time in thirty years — four thousand pounds of second-grade Assembly salt, pressed to the Compact mould, owed at the far rim at the Assembly's own assessed price by the master personally and not by the firm — and went up in a stack beside the cairn, white on brown, and by the fifth course nobody could look at it without the mask.

Ysoly turned the tally past heads, past water drawn, to the ballast face, and cut a hundred where the hands could watch her do it. Then she turned it to the fourth face and cut it again.

## The Figure Unchanged

They were nine feet down by the third hour of the morning and Hanei was in the hole.

It had gone easily to four — the brown came off the spade in slabs and the salt under it broke to the blade and went up in a bucket faster than two people could carry it away — and then the grey clay started and the whole thing slowed to inches and shoulders. They cut it four feet square, because Denet said a round hole in soft ground closes on you and a square one tells you when it is going. Lyddan dug and Hanei filled and Bris hauled, then Bris dug and Hanei hauled, then Hanei dug.

She had spent a whole night on the rope at Sixty Wells, paying hemp over dressed stone and not once allowed the bucket. She had the bucket now. It was not better.

At a little after nine feet the clay changed under the blade. It went from grey to the colour of a bruise, and heavier, and it came off the spade in a way that was almost soft, and then the hole began to take her boots.

"Out," said Ysoly, from the rim.

"It's coming—"

"Out. Now, and say it back."

"Out now." Hanei went up the ladder of cut steps with the water over the welts of her boots and stood on the brown ground with her heart going.

It came in from the bottom and from the four sides at once. Not a spring and not a rush; a seeping, everywhere, the walls going dark from the foot upward, and a flat sheet forming across the bottom of a hole that eleven people had spent seven hours digging with two spades.

They stood round it with a lamp and watched it fill for a while, because there was nothing to do about it and nowhere else to be.

By the time the lamp had burned down to the last of its oil there were two feet of water standing in the ground at the centre of the Meniscus, and it had a lamp-flame on it, moving.

Denet floated the glass on it there in the hole, on a line, and read it, and said the number aloud without being asked, and put the glass back in the fleece, and then stood holding the case because there was still nowhere on the whole plain to set it down.

Sabra Onn came out at the turn of the last shift with a tin cup in her hand.

She had been at the pound all night and her sleeves were wet to the elbow and she did not look at any of them. She knelt at the rim, and dipped the cup, and drank it off, and knelt there.

Thirty-seven years of weighing it. Sixty stone lids and a taster's board and a woman on her knees in the white with a lantern, spitting into the dark. Four hundred people gone south past a rock with what they had, and the days after the Ferry that she did not describe.

Day twenty. She had walked over the twentieth day thirty-one times.

She dipped the cup again, full, and held it out at arm's length over the ground, and turned it over, and poured the whole of it away.

It went into the brown ground and the ground took it and there was a dark patch about the size of a hand, and then the dark patch was smaller, and then it was gone.

Hanei, who had been taught in twenty days that spilled water was close to a sin and had been made to say so back word for word, stood four feet away and said nothing at all.

"Say nothing about that," Sabra said.

"No."

"Not to her and not to anybody."

"Not to her and not to anybody."

Sabra Onn got up off her knees, which took her a moment at sixty-one, and went down to the pound with the lantern held low, and walked the line of the string in the last of the dark the way she had walked it every morning of thirty-one crossings, listening.

One. Then three or four. Then the whole pound at once, forty-one animals coughing a night's salt out of their nostrils in a noise like a wall coming down slowly.

She got to the end and stood there. Then she came back up the line, slower, and stopped at the fourth team, and put her hand flat on a grey-dun shoulder gone chalk-white at the flank, and stood with it a while.

Forty.

She came out into the grey light where the wagons were and where eleven people were roping up round a hole with two feet of water standing in it, and she said it to nobody, to the ground, the way she said it every dawn.

"Two gallons the head, half a gallon the hask. Eleven and forty-one. Forty-two and a half the day." She had the tin cup still in her hand. "Ten days to water."

"Onn," Hanei said. "It's forty. The near-side on four didn't husk."

"It is forty-one until it is down."

"And there's water. There's water in the ground a hundred paces off, I dug it, I'm standing in the boots—"

"Ten days to water," Sabra Onn said, "and the butts stay full, and I will hear the figure back."

Hanei said it back. All of it, word for word, in the voice she had never got right and had stopped trying to get right, standing on brown ground in the first of the light with wet clay to the knee.

Behind them, at the cairn, Iseul Fane finished picketing her animal and walked out along the stake line past nine numbered stakes and their spoil rings, and past a stack of a hundred blocks of second-grade salt standing white on the brown, and came to the edge of a square hole four feet across, and stopped, and looked down into it for a long time at water standing within the bounds of the Meniscus.
