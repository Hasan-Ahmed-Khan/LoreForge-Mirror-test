# 5. Salt Law

> The Centre, days twenty-one to twenty-three. Iseul Fane stands at the rim of a hole eleven people dug in one night and takes the record in order -- who ordered it, who dug it, with what, to what depth -- and then recites the Third Article at them: every well within the bounds is the Wardenry's to open. A hole is not a well; a hole nine feet deep with water standing in it and a woman drinking out of it at dawn is something nobody has ever had to name. She tells them she has cause to break the tile, tells them so out loud in front of the hands, and gives them the second proviso for nothing: ground, not paper -- the packet goes off the ground in the hand that holds it. That night at the cairn she opens the waxed sleeve a third time in three days and reads both Articles against each other for the first time in a hundred and sixty-six years: the Second lets her break the tile on a spoken cause, the Fourth lets a master with a whole tile require the survey be begun within a season, and whichever sentence is spoken first governs. She is the only mouth between the rims that can speak either, and she tells Ysoly so rather than spring it. On the twenty-second the near-side leader on four goes down in the traces with the staggers and is cut out where she lies, and eleven people find they can bury a hask for the first time in the history of the Road, because the ground at the Centre takes a spade -- and the argument runs across the grave, and it runs by age. On the twenty-third Sabra Onn draws the hole to the clay four times and fills every butt on nine wagons, one thousand and eighty gallons out of a four-foot square, which is the volume the packet could never give; Ysoly cuts it on the water face in front of her own hands, where under the fifth head of the Compact it becomes a book of account admissible at either rim and beyond the reach of any seal. Oreth Vask rides in at dusk on four hask with the dust of thirty-six wagons standing on the north beacons behind him, prices everything, reads the word 'begun' back at them, and does not finish the last sentence. Then Iseul unwraps a lead disc that has been in cloth since RR 191, names Oreth Vask and Sabra Onn as her two witnesses, drives the pin, speaks the Fourth Article onto the ground, and names Denet Rasca the finder -- so that the Wardenry owes itself. Then she speaks the cause under the Second Article and puts her hand out for the tile.

## Every Well Within the Bounds

The Warden had been standing at the rim of the hole since first light, and she had not once put a boot inside the ring of spoil.

Ysoly Tamm came out to her at the second hour with the licence book under her arm and did not open it. Behind them the string stood roped and yoked and had stood roped and yoked for an hour, because nobody had told it to go and nobody had told it to stop.

"I will take it in order," Iseul Fane said. "Who ordered it dug."

"I did."

"Name the ones who dug it."

"Lyddan. Bris. The apprentice. Both Kaddals. Rooe on the buckets after midnight, Sennet on the steps, and Vesh went down twice and came up again because his knee is gone." Ysoly said it flat and whole. "I stood on the rim and told them when to come out."

"With what."

"Two spades off wagon seven and a hide bucket off the surveyor's rig."

"To what depth."

"Nine feet and a hand, four foot square, because a round hole in soft ground closes on you and a square one tells you when it is going." She looked down into it. "There were two feet of water in it at the third hour of the night and there are two feet in it now. It has stopped coming up and it has not gone down."

Iseul looked at that for a while, the way a person looks at a figure that has been added twice.

Then she said, "'Every well within the bounds is the Wardenry's to open, to close and to ration.' Third Article, original to the year one. I read you the whole of it across a table at Sixty Wells with your water-master standing behind you, and you told me to say the part I was not saying, and I said it."

"It is a hole."

"It is nine feet deep, it has water standing in it, and a woman drank out of it at dawn."

Sennet had come round the far side of the spoil ring with his arms folded, which was how he said everything. "There is a difference between a hole and a well, Warden."

"Name it."

"A well has a collar on it. Sixty Wells has sixty of them, cut and packed black. The Cold Well has dressed stone twenty feet down and a windlass house over the top and a man with a padlock."

"Then a well is a thing somebody paid for," Iseul said, "and the Wardenry's authority runs to the water that cost money and stops at the water that did not."

Nobody had an answer to that, and Denet Rasca, who had been standing at the second stake since before Ysoly came out, said, "It runs the other way as well. You should have it both ways before you decide."

"Say it."

"If that is a well, then every hole any man ever puts in this ground is yours to open and close, from here to either rim and for ever, and the Wardenry has this morning acquired eight hundred miles of wells that do not exist yet." He stopped and began the sentence again, which he did. "I'm sorry. And if it is not a well, then there is nothing in the statute, or in the Compact, or in any counting-house at either rim that stops the next man digging one twenty paces off, and the one after him, and there is no instrument anywhere for making either of them stop."

"Yes," Iseul said, and that was the whole of what she said about it.

She turned to face the wagons, so that the hands under the awning line could hear her without appearing to listen, which was where all of them were.

"Master Tamm. I have cause to revoke your licence, and I am going to say so on the ground, in front of your people, this morning, while there is nothing standing on it yet. A thing that can be done to you in a week without warning is not law. It is weather." She let that lie. "You opened water within the bounds without a Warden to open it. It is not a doubtful cause and there is no reading of the Third Article that saves you from it, and I want you to have heard me say it in the daylight rather than work it out at a fire."

"Then say the rest," Ysoly said.

"There is no rest yet."

Hanei, at the guy-rope end with her hands still black from the clay, said, "The packet," and then went the colour of a fired tile, because she had not meant to say anything at all.

Iseul put her hand inside her coat and took out the waxed sleeve, which she had carried four years without opening and had now opened on two days running, and every hand on the Tamm Line watched her do it a third time.

"'Provided also, that the seal lie upon the ground and the water and upon nothing else. It shall not reach to survey, level, note, log nor instrument, nor to the person carrying the same, nor compel the showing of them. Papers are the goods of him that holds them, and go off the ground in his hand.'" She folded the sleeve and put it away. "Whatever I do here, I cannot take a leaf of it, I cannot open it, and I cannot make any of you show me what is in it. That is the whole of what I have to give you and I am giving it to you before anybody asks."

"And the water?"

"Until I speak, the water is yours and you may draw it. After I speak it is held in trust and a cup out of that hole is against the Wardenry, and I will act on it, and I will be sorry." Iseul looked north up the beacon line, where there was nothing, and would be something in two days. "I will speak it before the joint tile comes through. I am telling you that so that nobody here can say afterwards that they were not told."

She went to fetch her animal.

Ysoly Tamm stood a moment at the rim of a hole with the licence book under her arm. Then she went down the string with her hand out, touching every butt at the hoop as she came to it. Seventeen wet. Nineteen dry and stopped.

She got to the ninth wagon, turned round, and counted them again.

## Two Sentences, One Mouth

She had a lamp on the cairn and the waxed sleeve open on her knee, and she was reading it by a light that had to be shielded from nothing, because there was no wind at the centre of the plain at night and never had been.

Ysoly came up out of the dark and stood on the other side of the stone.

"Sit down, master. There is one flat stone and you are fifty-four."

"I'll stand."

"Then we will both stand and neither of us will be honest about it," Iseul said, "and I have not the time." She waited until Ysoly sat down on the spoil and then sat down herself on the other side, with the lamp between them, and turned the sleeve round so that the writing faced the light and not her.

"I am going to read you two sentences. They are a hundred and sixty-six years apart and I do not believe anybody has ever had to read them in the same evening."

"Read them."

"Second Article. Original to the year one. 'A Warden of the Third Rank or above may revoke a licence upon the road, by breaking the tile in the holder's hand and in his sight, speaking the cause before he breaks it and before one witness that can be named; and the halves shall be kept and carried back to the roll at Bell Harrow, and the cause entered there beside the roll number.'" She did not look up. "'A Warden below the Third Rank may take up a tile and report it, and may not break it.'"

"I have heard the first half of that in a licence office."

"You have heard the part they tell an apprentice." Iseul turned the leaf. "Fourth Article, sealed in the year one hundred and sixty-seven, the last proviso. 'Provided lastly, that where the master of a lawful crossing stands upon sealed ground, holding his tile whole, he may require of the Warden, before the same two witnesses, that the survey be begun before the turn of the season next following; and the Warden shall enter the requirement upon the roll beside the seal, and the Wardenry shall appoint the surveyor and bear the cost of him.'"

She stopped there, and then read the rest of it anyway, because she was a woman who did not leave the second half of a sentence in her mouth.

"'A requirement so made is not undone by the after-breaking of the tile. But a tile broken before the speaking of it is no licence, and he that holds no licence may require nothing.'"

The crust ticked somewhere out beyond the stakes as it let go of the day.

"Say it plainly," Ysoly said.

"If your tile is whole when you speak, the requirement stands, and it stands even if I break the clay four seconds afterwards, because the men who wrote the proviso could see that far and put it in the text. If I speak the cause and break the tile first, then there is nobody standing on that ground with a licence, and there is nothing to require, and the ground is held the way Ferrant's ground has been held for fourteen years." Iseul folded the sleeve shut with her thumb inside it. "Two sentences. Whichever is spoken first governs. And I am the only mouth between the rims that can speak either of them, which I did not arrange and cannot help."

Ysoly Tamm sat with her hands on her knees and did not move for some while.

"Then why in God's name are you telling me?"

"Because if I do not tell you, then what happens on this ground in two days is not law. It is a woman on a horse arriving early." Iseul said it without heat, the way she said everything. "I have been twelve years at the floor of this Article. I have never once had to know what I would do, and I have never asked for a seat at Bell Harrow, and I have thought about that more in three days than in the twelve years."

"You could ride on. You have found eleven stakes and a bucket of wet clay."

"I have found water standing within the bounds of the Meniscus with a hundred blocks of Assembly ballast stacked beside it, and eleven people camped on it, and thirty-six wagons two days behind." Iseul turned her head. "Now hear the other side of it said properly, because you will get it badly from somebody else. In the year one hundred and sixty-six a man chalked nine numbers on a board at first light and went to his breakfast, and four hundred people went south out of that field on what he had chalked, and something over two hundred of them never reached the Ferry, and the ones who did came past this cairn on the twentieth day with brine in the butts. Your water-master filled a barrel off that board. The Fourth Article is the answer the rim gave to it, and the answer was: nobody drinks unsurveyed water within the bounds ever again. If that hole goes brack in a season and four hundred people have arranged a crossing round it, you will get the wells again with no wells to ration."

"Ferrant," Ysoly said.

"Ferrant."

"Ninety feet, forty miles out, assayed on his own spoil-heap in one morning by two Wardenry men with a Wardenry glass, capped with plain-salt and mortar inside the week. Fourteen years and no surveyor appointed. He asked in the year one hundred and ninety and again in the year one hundred and ninety-three, and he died asking."

"Yes," Iseul said. "That is why the fourth proviso is in the Article. It is the only limit in it with teeth, and it was put there by men who had watched what a Wardenry does with an indefinite hold." She looked at the lamp. "And it rides on a fired tile the size of a palm, and I am the person who can break the tile. I have been on this ground since the third hour of yesterday morning trying to find the reading in which those two facts are not both true, and there is not one."

Out past the stakes something moved in the pound and settled, and a single hask coughed and did not go on coughing.

"There is one more thing and I would rather say it tonight than in front of two hundred people." Iseul put the sleeve back inside her coat. "The Article says the Wardenry shall pay the finder the worth of the ground at the last assessed price of salt. It has never once been invoked where the finder was a Warden. Whoever I name, I have to name aloud, and the naming goes on the roll at Bell Harrow beside the seal."

She got up, and put her hand on the bronze plug in the top of the cairn without appearing to know she had done it, the way two centuries of people had.

"So somebody is going to have to tell me who found this water," she said. "And I have thought about it for a day and a night, and I do not like either answer."

## Forty

She went down at the ninth hour of the morning, four hundred paces short of the pound, walking a slow curve out of the line of the string with her head low and her eyes closed against nothing.

"Cut her," Pell Corrow said, and had the knife out of his boot before he had finished saying it, because a downed animal still in harness takes the wagon over and the wagon takes the string, and everybody on the Meniscus learns that before they learn the ration.

She was the near-side leader on four, and she was nineteen years old, and Ysoly Tamm had crossed the plain on her twenty-two times.

They got the traces off her and the yoke-chain clear and stood back, and she lay on the brown ground with her flank going chalk-white where the salt of three weeks had dried on it, and coughed once, which was worse than not coughing.

"Onn," Ysoly said.

"There is nothing to be done for the staggers and there never was." Sabra was already on her knees at the animal's head, with her hand flat on the shoulder where a drover puts it. "She had a half-gallon every day of this crossing and she drank at every lid I cut, and she is nineteen. Do not go looking for a fault in the water. There isn't one."

It took the better part of an hour. Nobody talked through it and Pell talked less than anybody.

Then Bris said, "Where do we leave her?"

On the crust you leave them. There is no other answer and there never has been: the plain is grey-white salt over grey clay to the depth of a man's arm, and a spade turns on it, and every drover between the rims has walked away from an animal lying out under the glare with the ridges going away from her in plates. It is the thing about the trade that nobody at a rim ever asks about twice.

Hanei looked at the ground.

"We can dig," she said.

They dug. It took five of them the rest of the morning and the whole of the afternoon and they broke the handle of the second spade doing it, and the brown came off in slabs and the salt under it broke to the blade and the grey clay below that was the worst work any of them had ever done, and it was the first grave any hask had ever had on the Meniscus in two hundred years of the Road.

The argument ran across it while they worked, because there was nowhere on nine wagons to have it and there never had been.

"She speaks tomorrow," Vesh said, from the bottom, handing a spadeful up. "Then it is sealed and we are on the road the day after and we are at the Cold Well in ten days and at the far rim in twenty-one, and we get paid, and I have driven for three masters and two of them lost their wagons and I have never once been paid at a rim by a woman still trading."

"You have been paid every crossing you ever made with this Line," Sabra said.

"I have. That is why I am saying it out loud instead of behind her."

"And the hole?" Hanei said.

"Capped. Plain-salt and mortar, the way they did Ferrant. I am sorry, girl. You dug it and I helped you and it is the best thing I have ever been part of and it is going under a mortar cap on Thursday."

Rooe said, "Say the rest of it," and Vesh said, "There is no rest of it. I am forty-six," and went back to digging.

"I am thirty-four," Pell Corrow said.

Nobody had asked him.

"My paper at Bram's is sixty-four blocks and the interest at a quarter, and it is written in blocks, because everything on that quay is written in blocks. If salt goes to a tenth of what it was in the spring, sixty-four blocks is a season's wage and a bit, and I clear it in one crossing, and Enna does not go on that paper at fourteen." He had not stopped working. "That is the whole of my interest and I have said it before and I will say it as often as anybody wants, and I would rather you all had it off me than worked it out about me."

"And your wage?" Sabra said. "Paid out of what?"

"Yes," Pell said. "I know."

Lyddan was twenty-two and Hanei was nineteen and neither of them said anything, and both of them had been digging since the morning without being told to, which was the whole of what they had to say and everybody under the tents heard it.

Sennet stood at the edge with his arms folded. "I would still want to know what happens at the Cold Well."

"Then say that," Ysoly said.

"I am saying it. It is not an answer."

"No."

They got her in at the last of the light and filled it, and it took less time to fill than it had taken to open, which is a thing about graves that none of them had known because none of them had ever dug one out here.

Hanei stood over it afterwards, and beside it, in the brown ground, four feet from the turned earth, was the print of a splayed foot the size of a dinner plate, standing there with the horn rim of it legible, because this was the only ground between the rims that kept anything.

"Master," she said. "What are you going to do?"

"Count the pound," Ysoly said. "Forty. Say it back."

"Forty."

"Say it to Onn at dusk and she says it to me and I cut it, and if you are ever wrong you come to me before somebody else comes to me." Ysoly went past her towards the wagons without stopping. "That is what I am going to do tonight, apprentice, and it is the only thing I have got that I am sure of."

## The Water Face

"Two gallons the head, half a gallon the hask. Eleven and forty. Forty-two the day." Sabra Onn said it to nobody in the grey light, the way she had said it every dawn for thirty-one crossings, so that afterwards no one could claim it had not been said. "Ten days to water. Four hundred and twenty. And I am standing on four hundred and twenty-five."

Nobody answered her. Nobody ever did.

"That is not a grace," she said. "That is a dare."

Then she went down the string and unstopped the first butt and poured it out on the ground.

It was hard brack out of a rock cistern, ten days old, bought at a block the two hundredweight in a dry season on the only rock between the rims, and it came out of the hide in a rope as thick as a wrist and hit the brown ground and stood in a sheet and then began to go down.

Nobody said one word to her. Vesh took his hat off and put it back on. Hanei, who had been taught in twenty days that spilled water was close to a sin and had been made to say so back word for word, went and unstopped the second butt herself.

Four thousand two hundred and fifty pounds of water went into the ground at Benchmark One before the third hour of the morning, and the ground took all of it, and by the time the last butt was up-ended there was no mark left anywhere the first had gone.

Then they filled.

It took from the third hour to dusk and it took all of them. Two hide buckets on a doubled line, a chain of people from the rim to the wagons, and the butts coming down off the slings four to a wagon and going back up full. The brown ground round the hole went to a trodden slick and then to a mire and then to something a boot came out of with a sound, and by noon there were eleven people working in wet clay to the knee in the middle of the driest place any of them would ever stand in.

They drew it to the clay in a little under two hours. There was a moment when the bucket came up with a pint in it and grit, and Sabra Onn stood at the rim of a hole she had emptied and looked at the bottom of it, and nobody breathed.

Then it came back.

It came back the way it had come in the first place, from the bottom and the four sides at once, the walls going dark from the foot upward, and by the middle of the afternoon there were two feet standing in it again and they went back to work.

Denet Rasca sat on the spoil ring with a boring log across his knee and did nothing at all except watch the water and mark the leaf.

"Four times," he said, at the end of it. "Drawn to the clay four times and recovered four times, and the fourth was no slower than the first. Two hundred and seventy gallons a draw, and a little over two hours to make it again, and I am rounding down every figure in that sentence because it is the first time anybody has had them and I would rather be under."

"Say what it means," Ysoly said.

"It means a four-foot hole at the Centre of the Meniscus will yield a thousand gallons a day to two buckets and a rope and it will do it tomorrow as well." He set the pencil down. "I'm sorry. I should say the rest, because it is the part I have not been able to write for five months. A boring gives you a depth and a quality. It cannot give you a volume, and the packet does not claim one, and any advocate at either rim would have taken the packet apart on exactly that in an afternoon. Reachable is not plentiful." He looked up. "Eleven people with two spades and one night have just measured the thing the survey could not. That is the number, master. It has been made in front of everybody standing on this ground and it has not been written anywhere."

Ysoly Tamm stood in wet clay to the knee at the centre of the plain and thought about that for a while.

Then she took the tally-stick out from under her coat.

"Corrow. Get everybody. Nobody goes to the pound until this is cut."

They came, in the order people come to a thing they have been sent for in daylight, and stood round in a ring in the mire with their hands hanging, and Ysoly turned a yard of black rim-oak past heads, past ballast, past the debt face, to the face cut for water drawn.

"One thousand and eighty gallons out of the ground at Benchmark One, on the twenty-third day of a summer crossing, drawn four times to the clay and recovered four times, at two hundred and seventy the draw." She cut it while she said it, and she said it twice, and the second time she said it slowly enough for a nineteen-year-old to get it word for word. "Cut by the master of a licensed crossing in the presence of her own hands. Say it back."

Hanei said it back. All of it.

"Now the surveyor will say why it is worth anything," Ysoly said, "because I have carried this stick for thirty years and I did not know until last night."

"The fifth head of the Compact of Weights," Denet said. "'A tally cut by the master of a licensed crossing, in the presence of her own hands, is a book of account at any weighbridge within the bounds -- as good as a ledger and admissible at either rim.'" He had his hands behind his back like a man being examined. "It is not a private note. It is an instrument. It is the same law that lets a weighbridge hold her to two gallons the head against a scale, and it works in both directions, and it has done since the year forty-seven."

"And the Article cannot reach it," Sabra said.

"'Papers are the goods of him that holds them, and go off the ground in his hand.'" Denet said it flatly. "The seal lies on ground and on water and on nothing else. That stick is neither. It goes south in her coat and it is evidence at Tallow Reach."

Sabra Onn wiped her hands down her thighs and looked at thirty-six butts standing full on nine wagons, one thousand and eighty gallons, more water than the Tamm Line had ever rolled out of anywhere in thirty years, and none of it bought, and none of it carried, and none of it weighed against anybody's cylinder.

"Twenty-five days," she said, to nobody. "Eleven and forty at forty-two the day. We can stand on this ground until the middle of next month."

"Say it properly," Ysoly said.

"Nobody has to leave here for anything." Sabra's voice did not change at any point. "Not for water. In thirty-seven years I have never once said that sentence and I did not expect to live to."

The dust came up on the northern beacons at the seventh hour, and it was not one rider's dust, and it stood in the air the way only a great many wheels can make it stand.

But the rider came in first, ahead of it, alone, on four hask with no wagon, at fifty-eight, with his face the colour of a fired tile and peeling along the cheekbones and his book in his coat. He came down off the animal at the cairn like a man getting off a roof and stood holding the saddle for a moment before he trusted his legs.

"Master Tamm," Oreth Vask said. "There are two hundred people nine miles behind me and they will be on this ground at first light, and I have ridden ten days twice this season, and I am too old for the second one." He looked past her at the hole, and at the stack of a hundred blocks standing white on the brown, and at thirty-six full butts on nine wagons.

Then he took out his book and opened it, and did not write anything in it at all.

## The Speaking of It

Vask did his arithmetic standing up, because there was nowhere at the Centre to sit that was not wet.

"I will be quick, and then I will stop, because a man who goes on after he is complete has stopped believing his own figures." He had the book open on his forearm. "The paper on your wagons falls due at the turn and is held two doors from my counting-house by people who have done as I asked for thirty-four years. It can be taken up on Thursday. The sum owing you at retirement is a promise from a body whose whole book is priced in salt, and if there is a working well at this cairn you will not get it, and neither will I. It can be paid in the autumn instead of owed. And a season's wage to each of the eleven. That was the fourth term at the Dry Ferry and it has not moved."

"And the fifth term," Ysoly said.

"Stands. Corrow's paper at Bram's, taken up and cancelled, and it does not turn on anything he does or says, and it did not at the Ferry either." Vask did not look at Pell while he said it, which was the one courtesy left on that ground. "I put that term in a book at Bell Harrow before your tile was fired, madam. I am not going to stand at the centre of the plain and pretend it is a thing I am offering you tonight."

Pell Corrow sat down on a spoil ring and put his hands on his knees and said nothing whatever.

"Now the part you want and will not like." Vask turned the book round, though it was too dark to read it. "The proviso says the survey be begun. Not finished, not reported, not certified. Begun. A man who walks out here in the autumn, sets one instrument on that ground and drives one stake has begun a survey, and there is no clause in the Article saying when he must be done. Ferrant's was never begun at all, which is the only reason anybody at a rim has ever noticed it." He shut the book. "You will hear that in a rim court in four years from somebody paid to say it, and I would rather you had it from a man who—"

He did not finish it.

"The requirement goes on the roll," Denet said. "Beside the seal, in her hand, at Bell Harrow, within forty days. A line of writing in a book that is read, naming the ground and the water and the requirer and the date. It is the only line that will ever exist about this place, because there is no fair copy of my survey anywhere in the world and there is not going to be." He was looking at Vask. "You bought that. Six lines, south, out of the depot's bag. I am not complaining of it. I am saying what is left."

Iseul Fane came up from the cairn with the cloth in her hand.

She unwrapped it in front of them and let the cloth fall over her wrist. The lead had been in it since the year one hundred and ninety-one and had taken the shape of the folds: a disc the width of a palm, dull, numbered on the roll at Bell Harrow, wired at the rim to an iron pin the length of a forearm. Every hand on the Tamm Line had watched her carry that saddlebag for twenty-three days.

"Two witnesses that can be named. Warden's practice is to name them first and I will keep to it. Oreth Vask, factor of the Salt Assembly at Bell Harrow. Sabra Onn, water-master, of Bell Harrow. You will be named on the roll and you may be called at either rim. If either of you refuses it now I will find two others and it will take me an hour."

Neither of them refused it.

"Not the surveyor, because a finder should not witness his own finding. Not the master, because she is a party." Iseul looked at Ysoly a moment. "And because in about four minutes she is going to need those two standing where they are."

She walked to the edge of the ground six paces west of the hole, where the brown was still hard, set the pin, and drove it with the flat of a spade through the crust to the collar, and it went in the way nothing on the plain goes in.

Then she stood up and put her hands at her sides.

"I have been twelve years at the floor of this Article and I have never once had to know what I would do." She was not speaking as an officer of anything, and every person there heard it. "The Wardens will hold this ground until the hazard is surveyed and lifted. I have read what that has meant once already, at a shaft forty miles out of a rim, to a man who asked twice and died asking. I am going to speak it anyway, and I would like it understood that I know exactly what I am doing."

Then she spoke it, and her voice went back to the register she read statutes in, which was flat and unhurried and belonged to nobody.

"Water rising or standing within the bounds is a hazard to the crossings and is held in trust by the Wardenry. Where such water is found, a Warden of the Third Rank or above, standing upon the ground and speaking before two witnesses, may declare the ground about it a hazard, and set a seal upon it, and hold it against all comers until the hazard is surveyed and lifted."

She set the disc against the pin and turned the wire down twice.

"I am Iseul Fane, Warden of the Third Rank, sealed to it at Bell Harrow in the year one hundred and ninety-one. I stand upon the ground at Benchmark One, within the bounds by the First Article, the salt lying to thirty-one inches at the thinnest of eleven bores. I declare this ground a hazard and I set the seal upon it, before Oreth Vask and before Sabra Onn, on the twenty-third day of the summer season of the year two hundred and three."

Nobody moved. On the northern horizon the fires of thirty-six wagons stood up in a line, nine miles off, and would be here at first light.

"The Wardenry shall pay the finder the worth of the ground at the last assessed price of salt." Iseul straightened up from the pin. "The finder is Denet Rasca, Warden-surveyor, sealed of the College of Levels, who augered eleven bores at this cairn in the seasons of two hundred and one to two hundred and three and floated a College glass on nine of them."

Vask's head came round.

"Yes," Iseul said. "The Wardenry owes itself. Nobody at Bell Harrow has ever had to write that sentence and they will have to write it in forty days, because I am going to carry it to them. And it is the only way a number of his gets onto any roll anywhere, and he knows it, and I will not pretend I did not think of that."

Denet Rasca stood at the second stake with his hands at his sides and did not say one word.

Then Iseul Fane turned round to face Ysoly Tamm across six feet of trodden brown ground, and her voice changed again, and it was the licence office at the head of the seventh quay with the tiles cooling on the rack behind her.

"Master Tamm. Under the Second Article, before one witness that can be named — Sabra Onn, water-master — the cause is this. On the twentieth and twenty-first days of this season you opened water within the bounds of the Meniscus without a Warden to open it, against the Third Article, by your own order and in your own presence, and you have this day drawn one thousand and eighty gallons out of it. The cause will be entered beside your roll number at Bell Harrow and you may sue it at the rim."

She put her hand out, palm up, into the space between them.

"The tile is broken in the holder's hand and in her sight. I do not take it off you. Hold it up."

Ysoly Tamm put her fist round four inches of fired clay on a thong at her neck, and thirty years of salt, and eleven people, and two hundred more nine miles behind her.

She lifted it.

Iseul's fingers closed over the back of her hand. Neither of them was holding anything yet, and the whole of the Tamm Line stood in the mire around a hole with a lead disc on a pin at the edge of it.

Ysoly opened her mouth.
