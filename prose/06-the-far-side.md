# 6. The Far Side

> The requirement is spoken first. At last light on the twenty-third day, with Iseul Fane's fingers over the back of her hand and the fired tile whole in it, Ysoly Tamm requires of the Warden -- before the same two named witnesses, Oreth Vask and Sabra Onn -- that the survey be begun before the turn of the season next following. Iseul accepts it, undertakes to enter it on the roll at Bell Harrow beside her own seal within forty days because the Article obliges her and because she believes in it, and then breaks the clay, cuts the thong and rolls the halves in the cloth the hazard seal has lain in since RR 191. The clock is running and it cannot be stopped: the Wardenry must appoint a College-sealed surveyor and bear the cost of him, there is exactly one inside three hundred and eighty-five miles and he is the man who wrote the survey they wanted buried, and if the survey is not begun the seal lifts of itself and the ground is free of it for ever. Vask prices both doors and finds they open on the same room. What it costs is everything else. A broken tile is no licence, so at first light the Tamm Line goes onto Sild's joint tile as hands, and two hundred people look down into fresh water at the centre of the Meniscus and get back on the boxes, because the ruin of one licence is the whole of the enforcement. Vask takes his letter to the Level House out of the south bag and hands it back; Denet Rasca folds it inside four lines of his own and files both, five months late and one post too late to save him, then cuts the packet open, carries forty leaves north to be appointed to survey his own finding, and puts leaf forty-one -- the water-table map, the only part of it any use to a person rather than an institution -- into Ysoly's hand. At the Swallet on the thirty-fourth day, in front of forty acres of glasswort and six people on a windlass, Sabra Onn tells her plainly what she has done to Aunis and her boy and the thirty in the beds and Rusk and Aske and a hundred and eighty hands and herself at sixty-one: a clock started for people who did not ask, who will not thank her, and who will walk. At Tallow Reach on the forty-fourth day the ballast is twenty-two blocks short and owed personally by a woman entered on the bill as a hand; the tally-stick is entered and referred to a rim court that will take four years, wages and all; the forty go into the outbound pound at four blocks a head, the twenty-two-crossing animals with the rest; Sabra will not count the crossing, because the water was not hers. A clerk at the Level House enters Denet's four lines and the letter lifting his seal out of the same bag in the same hour, and hands leaf forty-one back across the desk. Hanei asks what a pupil-leveller costs, gets the terms, and repeats them back word for word. The next morning, at the husking hour, with no string and no butts and no line, she stands on the red shelf and says the figure aloud to nobody, so that afterwards no one can claim it was not said.

## The Forty Days

She had spent thirty years saying figures out loud to men with slates, and that was the whole of the training she had for it.

"Warden Fane."

"Master."

"I am the master of a lawful crossing." It came out in the register she kept for a beam. "I am standing on ground you have this minute sealed. My tile is whole and it is in my own hand. Oreth Vask and Sabra Onn are standing where you put them."

"Go on."

"I require of you, before the same two witnesses, that the survey be begun before the turn of the season next following."

Out past the stakes the crust ticked once as it let go of the day.

Iseul Fane's fingers were still closed over the back of her hand, and she did not take them away.

"Say the ground."

"The ground at Benchmark One that you have sealed, and the water under it."

"Say the day."

"The twenty-third day of the summer season of the year two hundred and three."

"Oreth Vask."

Vask had shut his book and was holding it against his chest with both hands, which nobody there had seen him do. "I have heard it."

"Sabra Onn."

"I have heard it."

"Then it is required." Iseul said it the way she said everything, and it went out into the dark and did not come back. "The Wardenry shall appoint the surveyor and bear the cost of him. I shall enter the requirement on the roll at Bell Harrow beside my own seal, in my own hand, within forty days."

"You will write that yourself."

"The Article says the Warden shall. There is one Warden." Her voice did not move. "And I would write it if it did not say so."

She had not let go of the hand.

"One more. 'A requirement so made is not undone by the after-breaking of the tile.' Say it back, out loud, so that it has been said twice in front of the same two people."

Ysoly said it back.

At the guy-rope end Hanei said it too, under her breath and four seconds behind, because she had never once been able to help it.

"Now the other thing," Iseul said, "because I spoke the cause first, and I do not get to be sorry about the order of my own sentences."

She turned Ysoly's hand over so the tile lay flat and face up in the palm, the roll number standing under the salt-ash glaze where nothing could scratch it off and nothing could mend it. Then she put her own hand down over the top of it, and closed both, and leaned.

It made one sound.

It was not loud. Four inches of fired clay gives up in a single flat crack, like a dry rick going over in the next field, and every master on the Meniscus spends a working life listening for it in other people's yards. Hanei had never heard it. She heard it once.

Iseul cut the thong at the back of Ysoly's neck with a knife she did not have to look for, took the halves off it, and fitted them together to be certain both were there. Then she brought out the cloth the lead had lain in since the year one hundred and ninety-one, still folded to the shape of it, and rolled the halves in that, because it was empty now and it was what she had.

"The cause is entered beside your roll number at Bell Harrow, and you may sue it at the rim and be paid your loss if it fails." She put the cloth inside her coat. "It will not fail. You opened water within the bounds without a Warden to open it, by your own order and in your own presence, and I watched you do it. I would rather you did not spend four years and somebody's money finding that out."

Nobody moved.

"May I do the arithmetic out loud," Oreth Vask said, "or would you rather find it out over the winter?"

"Say it."

"The Wardenry must set a College-sealed surveyor on that ground before the autumn turns, and bear the cost of him. There is one College-sealed surveyor inside three hundred and eighty-five miles of where I am standing, and he is at the second stake with his hands at his sides." Vask did not turn round to look at him. "And if it does not begin, the seal lifts of itself, the ground is free, and it can never be declared again upon that water, and any man with a spade may have it. Thirty-four years in this trade, and I have never before been shown two doors and found that both of them open on the same room."

"And the finder," Sabra said.

"The Wardenry shall pay the finder the worth of the ground at the last assessed price of salt. Assessed by us." He said it without a flicker. "In the spring I would have called that four hundred blocks. By the time Bell Harrow gets round to paying him it will not buy a wagon and a team. That is not a threat, madam. I have stopped making them tonight. It is the only funny thing left in the whole business and I should like somebody to have heard me say it."

Ysoly Tamm stood with a cut thong hanging over her collarbone.

"I have nine wagons, forty head, eleven people and a passenger," she said, "three hundred and eighty-five miles from a rim, and no licence."

"Yes."

"Say the rest."

"'No wagon, beast or person shall pass the bounds unlicensed.'" Iseul looked north up the beacon line, where the fires of thirty-six wagons stood in a row nine miles off. "That comes onto this ground at first light under one master's tile, and I am a Warden of the Third Rank with a pen. I will endorse your people and your beasts and your wagons onto his bill in the morning if he will have you. He will have you. It will be the worst hour of your life and you will live through it, and eleven people will come off the salt, which is the only thing in the whole statute I have ever been certain about."

She went to see to her animal.

Ysoly went down the string in the dark with her hand out, touching every butt at the hoop as she came to it — thirty-six full, one thousand and eighty gallons, drawn lawfully out of the ground at Benchmark One on the last morning it had been lawful to draw anything.

She got to the ninth wagon, turned round, and counted them again.

## What the Wagon Carries

The joint tile took an hour and a half to come onto the ground, which is what two hundred people look like from underneath.

Hanei watched it come in from the box of the fourth wagon. The Tamm Line had been rung out of Bell Harrow on nine strokes and nobody in the streets had stopped for it. This lot had had the better part of an hour of bell.

The master's name was Sild, and Ysoly Tamm walked out to meet him with a cut thong on her collarbone and nothing on the end of it.

She got four words in.

"Yes," Sild said.

"I have not—"

"You have not finished and I am not going to make you." He was sixty, and he had bought into another man's tile at twenty-six because he could not carry the risk alone. "Eleven hands, nine wagons, forty head and a hundred and ninety blocks of Assembly ballast, entered on my bill under my roll number, with the Warden's endorsement. You take my ration, my order of march and my dawn. Your water-master answers to mine."

"She will not like that."

"Mine will not like it either and neither of them gets a say." He looked past her at the hole. "Ysoly. You have stood in a yard at Bell Harrow and told me for thirty years that a master who buys into a joint tile is a woman renting out her own road. I have thought about that sentence in the dark more often than you would believe. I am not going to enjoy one minute of this, and I want it said between us before we start."

Iseul Fane wrote the endorsement on the tailgate of the fourth wagon and stamped it, and it took her four minutes, and it was the last lawful thing anybody ever did about the Tamm Line.

They came past the hole all morning, in ones and threes, the way people go to look at a thing there is nothing to be done about. Four feet square, nine feet down, two feet of water in it with the sky sitting on top, and six paces west a lead disc wired to an iron pin driven to the collar.

Sild put his hand on the pin and did not touch the disc.

"Anybody who takes a cup out of that walks to Tallow Reach," he said, to nobody in particular, in a voice that carried the length of a wagon train. "The Wardenry holds it in trust. I have two hundred people, one tile, and a woman standing forty feet from me with a cut thong at her neck, and that is the whole of my argument, and I have never had a better one."

Two hundred people looked down into fresh water at the centre of the Meniscus and got back on the boxes.

"The salt is goods," Iseul said, when Vesh asked her. "The seal lies upon the ground and the water and upon nothing else. Have it off my ground by noon."

So the last work the Tamm Line ever did as the Tamm Line was carrying a hundred blocks of another firm's ballast back between the axles they had come off three days before, in the flat white of the morning, in front of two hundred people with nothing else to watch. Ysoly cut the hundred off the debt face where the hands could see her do it. It was the only debt on that stick she would ever discharge.

The depot's south bag came off the joint tile's second wagon at noon, and Oreth Vask was standing over it with the flap open when Denet Rasca came across the ground to him.

He had the letter in his hand. Six lines, sealed, to the keeper of the roll at the Level House.

"I could take it out," Vask said.

"Yes."

"I have been standing here eleven minutes working out whether it helps me. Lift your seal and the Wardenry cannot appoint you; if it cannot appoint you it cannot begin; if it cannot begin, the season turns and the seal lifts of itself and the ground is free for ever." He turned the letter over. "So it does not help me. It helps you, and it ruins me a year sooner, and I have been standing here eleven minutes anyway."

He held it out.

Denet Rasca took his own destruction out of a man's hand and did not open it, and stood holding it about as long as a glass takes to settle.

Then he sat down on the tailgate with a borrowed pen and wrote four lines. Surveyor. Ground. Date. One line of result.

He read them aloud, because Ysoly was behind him and Ysoly did not like a man reading to himself. "'Denet Rasca, sealed of the College of Levels. The centre of the Meniscus at Benchmark One and eleven bores east of it. The seasons of two hundred and one to two hundred and three. Fresh water under the crust at between nine and fourteen feet, floated against the reference brine; nine bores of eleven.'"

"It is five months late," Ysoly said.

"It is five months late, and it is entered by a man whose seal comes off him in the same post, which makes it worth nothing to anybody who reads it." He folded his four lines, folded Vask's six inside them, put both in the south bag and closed the flap. "If the number does not go on the roll it never happened, and I have had five months of finding out what that is like."

He had the packet out after that, Pell Corrow's pale crooked thread still across the head of it, and cut the thread with the same knife he had used at Sixty Wells. Then he counted forty leaves off the top and squared them on his knee.

The last was folded in four and larger than the rest.

"Leaf forty-one. The water-table map." He put it in Ysoly's hand. "Forty leaves prove a thing to a body that keeps rolls. This one tells a person where to put a spade, and it is the only part that is any use to somebody who is not an institution. 'Papers are the goods of him that holds them, and go off the ground in his hand.'"

"I carry nothing I cannot name on a bill," Ysoly said.

"You have no bill."

She looked at it a while. Then she put it inside her coat, next to a yard of black rim-oak that had stopped being a book of account at last light on the twenty-third day.

They went north at the second hour of the afternoon, which is the hour nobody travels, because Iseul Fane had forty days to the roll and had decided to spend fifteen of them. Ysoly weighed their water herself, off her own butts, against a cylinder borrowed from the cook wagon: two people, fifteen days, sixty gallons and the grace on it. She did not hurry any part of it, because it was the last provisioning she would ever do and nobody there was going to say so.

Denet Rasca went up on a hask with forty leaves against his body under his coat and the brass-cornered case on a strap across it, and got half a sentence out.

"Don't," Ysoly said.

He didn't.

## The Padlock

Ten days south of the Centre the beacon line bows west, and on the thirty-fourth day the Tamm Line came into the Swallet on somebody else's bill.

Hanei had been told about the cold and had repeated it back and had not believed it. It came up sixty feet out of river gravel on a windlass that never stopped, and it was cold, which nothing on the Meniscus is, and it stained the trough and the hands and the hide a permanent ochre.

Forty acres of glasswort under irrigation off the overflow, the only green between the rims. A pressing shed with the cakes stacked to the roof. A windlass house, the only roof for ten days in either direction. Two rooms with a strongbox in the floor, a toll-book, a Warden of the Fifth Rank named Brede, and the padlock, which is the actual instrument of government on the southern leg and which everybody out here has arranged a whole life around without thinking it strange.

The news had got there four days ahead of them, because news on the plain travels at the speed of two hundred people talking. Nobody said anything to them, and that was the part Hanei could not get over: thirty people in the only settlement on five hundred and fifty miles of road, all of them had done the sum before the wagons came down the bow, and not one said a word to a woman with a cut thong.

Sild paid the toll on forty-five wagons, two blocks apiece, and had the nine entered against the Tamm Line at Bell Harrow. Brede wrote it without comment, because he had a book and the book had a column for it.

Sabra Onn went out to the beds in the last of the light and Ysoly went after her, and Hanei, who had the count and had been told to bring it, followed and stopped forty feet short and heard all of it, and never afterwards pretended she had not.

"Aunis is cutting in the second bed," Sabra said. "She was twelve the first time I came through and she is thirty-nine and she has a boy of nine on the windlass. There are six on that windlass and they do nothing else and never have. There are thirty in the beds. Every one of them is standing in the middle of eight hundred miles of salt because this shaft is sixty feet deep and a Warden keeps a padlock for it."

"I know what the Swallet is."

"Then let me say the rest. I have had ten days on a box behind another woman's cook wagon to get it in order." She did not raise her voice at any point. "There is no fodder south of here and none north of here. There is one pressing shed and it is that one, and it is here because there is water at sixty feet and glasswort will drink the overflow. Put a hole every twenty paces from here to either rim and there is no toll and no padlock and no crew and no beds, and thirty people are standing at the centre of the plain with a shed. Nobody is coming out to give them anything. They will walk."

Ysoly said nothing.

"Rusk keeps a board at Sixty Wells for two blocks a crossing and it is the only thing he does. Aske has a toll-book. The man at the Ferry sells cistern water by weight and thinks he is hard done by, and fifty people on that rock eat because he does. Two hundred on the tile behind us, and a hundred and eighty are hands, and a hand is worth exactly what a crossing is worth." Sabra folded her arms. "I have been going down that list since the twenty-fourth day and it does not stop."

"Say the part about you."

"I am sixty-one. I have weighed water for thirty-seven years and I am better at it than anybody between the rims, and the whole of that is scarcity. There is no trade in weighing a thing nobody has to carry." She said it without any weight on it. "I will not be a water-master again. I knew that on the twentieth day before you did, and I told you to dig it anyway, and I would tell you again tomorrow."

"Then say what you came out here to say."

"I told you to dig it. I did not tell you to speak." Sabra turned round. "You stood on that ground and started a clock nobody can stop, on behalf of two rim-cities and thirty people in a glasswort bed and every soul on this plain who is not a counting-house, and not one of them asked you to. Most would have told you not to. You did not ask because you knew what they would say. You will not get a rim court and you will not get a season and you will not get thanked, and the ones who land soft out of it will be a broker's daughter and a factor's grandchildren, because it is always the people with paper who land soft."

The windlass ratchet went behind them, and went again.

"And I stood where the Warden put me and said I had heard it," Sabra said. "So do not go telling yourself at the far rim that Onn never forgave you. I witnessed it. My name goes on a roll at Bell Harrow beside her seal, and if I am called at either rim I will say the same words in the same order, because it happened and I was standing there."

Ysoly Tamm stood in front of forty acres of the only green on the Meniscus.

"I am not going to argue with any of it," she said.

"I did not think you would."

"I have turned it over for eleven days and I cannot find the sentence where I was wrong, and I cannot find the one where I was right, and both are true at once." She had a hand in her pocket, flat on a folded sheet. "I did it because there was a hole with water standing in it and a woman with a lead disc, and because Ferrant asked twice and died asking, and because I had four seconds. That is not a reason."

"No," Sabra said. "It is not a reason." And she went and worked.

Pell Corrow had not said anything since last light on the twenty-third. Eleven days of it. He roped and drove and mended other people's harness at other people's fires, and after four days the two Kaddals stopped noticing and after seven Hanei stopped being able to bear it.

At the pound gate in the dark he came up the line to Ysoly with a girth over his shoulder.

"Near-side leader on six has got a hot foot," he said. "Not lame. Hot. I'd walk her on the spare line to the rim."

"Do that."

"That's what I'm doing," Pell said, and went and did it.

That night Ysoly took the tally out from under her coat at the tailgate of the first wagon, where the hands could watch her do it, and turned it past heads, past water drawn, past ballast, to the fourth face. Then she turned it again, all four going by under her thumb, and then round once more, looking for the face to cut what Sabra Onn had said in front of the beds.

There was no face for it.

She put the knife back in her coat.

## Salt and Water

Tallow Reach comes up out of the plain the way it always has: a shelf of red rock where the old sea ended, a wall built to break the salt drift and not to stop anybody, and the rendering yards reaching twenty miles out to meet you.

They came up it on the forty-fourth day, three days late, in the middle of thirty-six other wagons, winched four at a time. The Tamm Line's nine went up eighth, ninth and tenth, in the order of somebody else's bill.

At the weighbridge they took off a hundred and ninety blocks of second-grade Assembly ballast against a bill for two hundred and twelve.

"Twenty-two short."

"Twenty-two at Sixty Wells, on ration, at a block the hundredweight, entered in Warden Aske's toll-book against the Bell Harrow roll." Ysoly had it in the licence book, and the licence book was no longer a licence book. "Owed by the master personally at the last assessed price, and I am not disputing a pound of it."

"And the master is."

"On this bill I am a hand."

He wrote that down. It took him some time to find where to write it.

Then she put the tally-stick on the counter, a yard of rim-oak gone black with thirty years of handling, and he read the water face with his finger under it, which is how a weighmaster reads.

"'One thousand and eighty gallons out of the ground at Benchmark One, on the twenty-third day, drawn four times to the clay and recovered four times, at two hundred and seventy the draw.'" He looked up. "Cut by the master of a licensed crossing in the presence of her own hands?"

"Cut at dusk on the twenty-third. The tile was broken at last light on the twenty-third."

He stood a while with his finger on the notch.

"Then I enter it and write beside it that it is referred, and it goes to a rim court. Four years. Whoever brings it pays for it. And every wage cut on the fourth face after last light on the twenty-third is referred with it." He pushed it back. "I am sorry, madam. I am not the man who decides and I would not want to be."

Vesh was hired onto a Reach line before noon and did not say goodbye, which was not unkindness: he had driven for three masters, two of them had lost their wagons, and he left before it could be done to him. Lyddan and Bris went to the outbound pound to be taken on for the northbound and were told to come back in a month.

The forty went into the pound on the second afternoon and were sold in a morning. A sound five-year-old is eleven blocks at Bell Harrow and four at Tallow Reach at the end of a crossing, and that difference is what a crossing costs before a hand is paid. The two that had crossed twenty-two times went for four apiece, the same as everything else, because there is no line in a pound-book for a hask a drover will walk across a yard to look at.

Ysoly Tamm stood at the rail and did not look away from any of it. Hanei stood beside her and did.

"Thirty-two," Hanei said afterwards, because she had been counting since Bell Harrow and it was the only thing she had to give anybody.

"Thirty-one," said Sabra Onn.

"Onn—"

"Thirty-one. I count the crossings where the water was mine." She had her bag on her shoulder and a season's wage that had been referred to a rim court. "That water came out of the ground. I did not carry it and I did not weigh it, and I will not have it on my count."

The Level House stands three streets up, where the shelf rock comes to the surface: one cut-stone hall and a short tower under a lead roof, founded straight onto the rock so that nothing in it moves. The joint tile's south bag went up to it on the second morning with everything else off the plain, and Ysoly went up after it with a folded sheet inside her coat.

The roll room smelled of lamp-black and tallow, because the pupils lodge over the rendering sheds and so does the roll. A clerk of about thirty was entering the post at a standing desk: four lines against the name of Denet Rasca, and out of the same bag in the same hour six lines from a factor of the Salt Assembly that would have his seal off him before the autumn.

He entered both in the same careful hand and did not appear to find it remarkable, which was the worst of it.

"Leaf forty-one of a survey," Ysoly said, and put it on the desk and opened it out. "The water-table map of the centre. It is in his hand, and I can name eleven witnesses to the ground it was drawn on."

He looked at it a long moment, and it cost him something.

"The roll takes findings from sealed surveyors. I cannot enter a sheet out of a caravan master's coat." He folded it back along its own creases, the way a man folds a thing that is not his. "If I could, this roll would be worth about what the hide market is worth, and then nothing on it would be worth anything, including his four lines. That is the whole of the College, and I am perfectly aware of what I am handing back."

She put it inside her coat.

Hanei had come up the three streets behind her and stopped inside the door, and when he was done she said, "What does it take?"

"To what?"

"To be a pupil."

"Sixteen, and seven years, because seven years is what a level costs." He was already back at the desk. "And candles, lamp-black and oiled paper, which the College does not find for you. Four blocks a year, near enough."

"Sixteen, and seven years, and four blocks a year for candles and lamp-black and oiled paper, which the College does not find for you," Hanei said.

"You are not sixteen."

"No."

She woke the next morning at the husking hour, because a body woken at the husking hour for forty-four days does not stop for a wall.

There was nothing to wake to. No one animal and then three or four and then the whole pound at once, coughing a night's salt out of its nostrils in a noise like a wall coming down slowly. No butts. No line. Below the salt wall the Meniscus lay out white to the curve of the eye, exactly as it had lain out from the window of the licence office at the head of the seventh quay, with no far side to it anywhere.

She knew what was under it now. That was the whole of the difference and it did not show from here.

She went and stood on the shelf where the rock comes up.

"Two gallons the head, half a gallon the hask," Hanei said, to nobody, to the shelf, the way it had been said every dawn for thirty-seven years so that afterwards no one could claim it had not been said. "Eleven and—"

She stopped.

Then she said the other one, the one that was still true, slowly, at the speed a person says a thing they want somebody to be able to repeat back.

"One thousand and eighty gallons out of the ground at Benchmark One, on the twenty-third day of the summer season of the year two hundred and three. Drawn four times to the clay and recovered four times. Two hundred and seventy the draw."

Nobody answered her.

Nobody ever did.
