# 6. The Far Side

> SALT AND ASH. The tile breaks first. At the rim of the hole on the twenty-third day, with the seal spoken and Denet Rasca named the finder, Ysoly Tamm has the requirement word for word and two named witnesses standing either side of her - and before she speaks she counts them again, because that is what she has done at the tailgate of the ninth wagon every dusk for thirty years. The clay lets go between her hand and the Warden's. She says the sentence over the halves and it is worth nothing: a tile broken before the speaking of it is no licence, and he that holds no licence may require nothing. No requirement, no season, no clock. Iseul Fane will not say whether she hurried, because she does not know. At first light the joint tile brings two hundred people past the hole to look down into it - exactly what Sabra Onn demanded on the rock, and worth nothing either - and the hole is filled with the Tamm Line's own hundred dumped blocks and held under a lead disc on an iron pin. The packet goes off the ground in the finder's hand, because the seal has no reach over paper, and the Line goes to Tallow Reach inside Master Sarn's tile. At the far rim the weighbridge takes the tally-stick and will not enter the water; the Level House takes the four lines Denet should have entered in the spring, and a fifth for the volume off Ysoly's notches, six hours before Vask's letter comes up in the depot bag and the College lifts his seal. The record ends complete, correct and public, and moves nothing, because to check a line of it a man must stand on ground the Wardenry holds and the Wardenry appoints the surveyor. Iseul enters the seal with eleven days to spare, writes that the Wardenry owes itself, and takes a seat at Bell Harrow to be in the room where the surveyor is appointed; he is not appointed. Denet is paid four blocks and a quarter for the ground and works out that he came out of the water at the centre of the Meniscus a quarter of a block to the good. Vask keeps every term he ever wrote, and Enna Corrow will not go on any paper at fourteen. Ysoly sells the Line at the arriving price, comes home on somebody else's way-bill as one woman with her own water, and gets the roof she wanted and nothing left in the room to count twice. The water stays in the ground. In the spring the crossings walk past the seal on the twentieth day and the string drinks brine off a cut lid nine feet above it, and at dusk Hanei goes down the line, counts the butts, turns round and counts them again.

## Whichever Is Spoken First

She had the sentence.

Iseul Fane had read it to her at the cairn two nights before, twice, and Ysoly Tamm had carried it since the way she carried a figure — under the mask against the wheel noise, turning it until the words went smooth.

I require of the Warden, before these two witnesses, that the survey be begun before the turn of the season next following.

Before these two witnesses. The Warden had stopped on that and said it again, because the proviso wanted the same two who had been named for the seal and not any two people standing about in a mire.

She looked to her right. Oreth Vask, with the book shut on his thumb and his face the colour of a fired tile.

She looked to her left. Sabra Onn, sleeves wet to the elbow, the tin cup still in her hand.

Two.

Then Ysoly Tamm did the thing she had done at the tailgate of the ninth wagon every dusk for thirty years, and had done at a weighbridge in the kiln smoke on the morning this crossing began, and would have told anyone who asked was not a habit but the trade itself.

She looked again. Vask. Onn.

Two.

The clay let go between her hand and the Warden's.

It was not loud. Fired clay in two fists does not crack; it gives once, dry, like a knuckle, and then there was a weight in her palm that was two weights and neither of them the right shape. The break had gone across the roll number, so that half the Wardenry's stamp lay under the glaze in her hand and half of it in Iseul Fane's, and the broken faces were white and coarse and had never been glazed at all.

Nine miles north the fires of thirty-six wagons stood up in a line and knew nothing whatever about it.

Ysoly said it anyway.

She said the whole of it, correctly, in the flat voice she kept for figures, before Oreth Vask and before Sabra Onn, standing in wet clay to the knee with two pieces of a licence in her fist: that she required of the Warden that the survey at Benchmark One be begun before the turn of the season next following.

Iseul Fane let her finish. That was the one thing she did.

"'A requirement so made is not undone by the after-breaking of the tile,'" Iseul said. "'But a tile broken before the speaking of it is no licence, and he that holds no licence may require nothing.'"

"I know what it says. You read it to me."

"I did, and I am going to say the rest out loud here rather than let you hear it in four years off a man paid to say it. There is nobody standing here holding a licence. There is no requirement, and no season, and no clock. The ground is held until the hazard is surveyed and lifted, and the Wardenry appoints the surveyor."

"Would you have entered it?"

"Yes."

Ysoly turned her hand over and looked at the halves lying in it.

"Did you hurry it?"

The crust ticked out past the stakes, letting go of the day, and one hask coughed in the pound and did not go on coughing.

"I have been asking myself that since my hand closed," Iseul said. "I will be asking it in ten years. So: I do not know. The Second Article says the cause is spoken and then the tile is broken, and it gives no interval. I do not know what my hand was doing."

Denet Rasca had come up from the second stake. He got as far as "Is there any—" and stopped, because there was not; and then, being the man he was, took the pencil out of his coat, and stood holding it over nothing at all, and put it back.

"There is nothing," he said. "I have been looking since she started speaking. Not in the statute and not in the Compact."

Oreth Vask opened his book and wrote it all, without hurry, the way he had done it to her on a quay for nineteen years: the seal, the witnesses, the finder, the cause, the break, the requirement spoken after. The last thing he put down was the hour.

"Master Tamm. I am sorry that it went in the order that—"

He did not finish it. He never finished them.

Sabra Onn, named on the Wardenry's roll that evening twice over — once as witness to the sealing of the water and once as witness to the breaking of the tile — said nothing whatever, and went on saying nothing for eleven days.

Iseul Fane put her hand out, palm up, into the space between them a second time.

"The halves are kept and carried back to the roll at Bell Harrow, and the cause entered beside your roll number. I do not take them off you. Give them to me."

Ysoly gave her the two pieces.

The Warden took out the cloth the lead disc had come out of an hour before — wrapped round it in a saddlebag since the year one hundred and ninety-one, and empty now — and folded the halves into it, and put it away.

"I will enter that you spoke the requirement, and the hour of it, and that the tile was broken before you spoke," she said. "That is not the requirement. It will not run a clock and it will not appoint a surveyor, and no clerk at Bell Harrow will read it twice. I am telling you what it is worth so that you do not go south believing it is worth something else."

Hanei stood at the guy-rope end with the clay dried grey to her elbows and understood, the way a person understands a thing years before they can say it, that she had watched the whole of it and had not seen anything happen. A woman had looked at two people, and looked at them again. That was the whole of what there had been to see.

Ysoly Tamm went down the string with her hand out, touching every butt at the hoop as she came to it.

Thirty-six wet. Nothing dry and stopped anywhere on nine wagons: one thousand and eighty gallons out of the ground at the centre of the Meniscus.

She got to the ninth wagon, turned round, came back up the line and counted them again.

There was nothing wrong with the count. There never had been.

## What the Wagon Carries

They came on at first light with the dust standing over them, and the sound arrived before the wagons did: thirty-six sets of iron tyres on crust, two hundred people, a cook wagon with its pots slung, a taster's mule. After twenty-four days of eleven voices over wheel noise it came across the brown ground like weather.

Master Sarn brought his string up short of the stake line and walked in. He was a heavy, careful man of sixty with a joint tile at his neck and eleven outfits and their passengers bought in under it, and he had gone out of Bell Harrow four days behind the Tamm Line for thirty years.

Iseul Fane met him at the pin and read him the declaration standing where she had spoken it: the ground about the water held in trust by the Wardenry, sealed before two named witnesses on the twenty-third day of the summer season, and held against all comers until the hazard is surveyed and lifted. Then she said the part he had come for.

"Your people may look. Looking is not drawing. Any person who puts a cup, a bucket or a hand into that hole is drawing water held in trust, and I will act on it."

"They will want to look."

"Then let them, and let them do it where I can see them. I would rather two hundred people saw the ground than heard about it at the Cold Well."

So they looked.

They came past in ones and threes for the better part of two hours — drovers, hands, passengers, the taster, the cook's boys — out along eleven numbered stakes, past a stack of a hundred blocks of Assembly salt standing white on the brown, to a square hole four feet across with two feet of water in it. Some of them knelt. A woman put her palm flat on the wet clay at the rim and took it off and looked at her hand. A man off the cook wagon said his father had walked over this on the twentieth day forty times.

Sabra Onn stood at the head of the stake line and watched two hundred people file past a hole in the ground and look down into it, which was the thing she had asked for under an awning on a rock thirteen days back. She got the whole of it. It was worth nothing, and she knew that while it was happening.

Then Iseul had it filled.

She gave the order at the ninth hour, evenly, without weight on it, because the Article said the ground was held and holding it meant nobody standing in a hole. The spoil went back first — eleven people had thrown it out in a night with two spades, and forty of Sarn's hands put it back in under half a morning — and then the salt on top of it: a hundred blocks of second grade, forty pounds apiece, laid flat in courses and broken in with the flat of a spade, which is the nearest thing to plain-salt and mortar anybody had out there.

They used the Tamm Line's own two spades. One had a handle spliced with harness thread, because Bris had broken it digging a grave for a hask two days before.

When it was done there was a patch of trodden white the size of a small room with a lead disc on an iron pin at the edge of it, and the glare would find it for a week and then it would be brown ground again. Four feet past the last stake was a mound with a splayed print beside it the size of a dinner plate, and nobody touched that at all.

"'Provided also, that the seal lie upon the ground and the water and upon nothing else,'" Iseul said afterwards at the tents, loudly enough for Sarn's people. "'It shall not reach to survey, level, note, log nor instrument, nor to the person carrying the same, nor compel the showing of them. Papers are the goods of him that holds them, and go off the ground in his hand.'" She folded the sleeve away. "The packet is the surveyor's. It is not cargo and it is not mine, and no Warden will ask for it at any post between here and the far rim."

Denet Rasca stood at the second stake and did not answer her, and Iseul Fane did not come within six paces of him for the rest of the morning.

Oreth Vask did the other business at the cairn with the book on his forearm.

"Nine wagons, forty draught, eleven hands, ninety blocks of second grade on the axles, bought into the joint tile at Master Sarn's rate for the run to Tallow Reach, entered on his bill under his roll number, the Assembly standing the difference." He turned a page. "The shortfall on the ballast is a hundred and twenty-two blocks, and it is owed at the far-rim weighbridge by the master personally and not by the firm. That is the Compact, and I cannot move it."

"And the terms."

"Stand. All five, unchanged, as they were written in a book at Bell Harrow before your tile was fired." He shut it. "I am aware of how this morning looks. I would ask you to believe I have not enjoyed an hour of it, and I know you cannot, and I would not either."

Master Sarn was decent about it, which was the worst of it. He offered her the head of his string for the twenty-one days, and said it where nobody could hear so that she could refuse it where nobody could hear, and she refused it.

Then she put her name to a paper she had refused for thirty years, and the Tamm Line went onto another master's way-bill.

They pulled out at the fourth hour past noon, thirty-six wagons and then nine, and Ysoly Tamm walked at the tail of it with a licence book under her arm and nothing to write in it.

In the locker under the seat of the fourth wagon, under a nineteen-year-old's blanket, a pound and thirteen ounces of oiled hide stitched shut with a drover's pale thread went south as freight on somebody else's bill; and Hanei, who had been given charge of it in a yard at Bell Harrow twenty-four days and three hundred and eighty-five miles back, put her hand flat on the locker lid the way she had every morning, and got up on the box.

## The Far Rim

The far rim came up on the forty-fifth day: a red line, then a shelf of rock with a wall on it, then the smell of a town that boils what it cannot sell. Tallow Reach has no quays. The wagons went up the shelf one at a time on a winch, and the crust let go of the wheels for the last time, and the world had a far side after all, and it was a poor one.

They weighed at the outbound bridge under the wall.

A hundred and twenty-two blocks short. Then Ysoly Tamm took a yard of black rim-oak from under her coat and laid it on the table, and the clerk turned it in his hands and read the four faces without being told which was which, because a weighbridge clerk has read a hundred of them.

Heads. Water drawn. Ballast. Debt.

"Twenty-two at Sixty Wells, eleven at the Dry Ferry, a hundred laid off at the centre," he said. "And this."

"Read it out."

"'One thousand and eighty gallons out of the ground at Benchmark One, on the twenty-third day of the summer season, drawn four times to the clay and recovered four times, at two hundred and seventy the draw.'" He looked up. "That is not a weighbridge matter."

"No."

"I will enter the ballast and the debt. Under the fifth head this stick is as good as a ledger and I will not argue with a notch. I cannot enter water that never went on a scale."

"Then write that I asked you to."

He looked at her a moment, and then did it, in the margin of the day's sheet: that the master of the crossing had asked, and had been told it was not a weighbridge matter. Ysoly thought about a woman on brown ground promising to enter that a sentence had been spoken too late, and about a figure called at dawn to nobody so that afterwards no one could claim it had not been said; and she put the stick back inside her coat.

The Level House stands three streets up where the shelf rock comes to the surface: one hall and a short tower of dressed stone under a lead roof, the only building in the reach with a face on it. Inside it is cold, and after forty-five days of hide and glare the cold came up through the boots. A datum is scribed across the floor, a slit cut in the north wall to let a stripe of noon in, and the place smells of stone, lamp-black and, faintly, tallow, because the pupils lodge over the rendering sheds and so does the roll.

Denet Rasca carried the packet up the three streets himself, set it on the roll room table, and cut Pell Corrow's pale thread with a knife.

The keeper of the roll was a woman of seventy with ink to the second knuckle, and she heard it all standing up: two summers, eleven bores, nine of them wet, the glass floated against the far-rim reference, the packet stitched in the spring, nothing entered, no fair copy anywhere, five months of carrying it.

"You know the rule."

"I know the rule. I drafted the entry twice and sent neither. I am asking to make it now."

"You are five months late and the ground is sealed."

"Yes."

"Then make it," she said, "because the roll takes the finding and not the man," and opened the leaf where there had been no line against his name since the spring, and gave him the pen.

Four lines. Surveyor, ground, date, one line of result: fresh water under the crust at Benchmark One, nine wet bores of eleven, struck between nine feet and fourteen, read fresh against the far-rim reference.

Then he asked for a fifth, and sent to the yards for Ysoly Tamm.

She came up in her salt-stiff coat and stood on cold stone holding a yard of notched oak while the keeper read a caravan master's water face aloud and wrote the volume into the College's book. Denet said the fifth head of the Compact for her, twice: a tally cut by the master of a licensed crossing, in the presence of her own hands, is a book of account within the bounds, admissible at either rim.

"She was licensed when she cut it," he said. "It was cut on the morning of the twenty-third. The tile went at dusk."

"Then the volume goes in," said the keeper, and it went in.

A boring gives a depth and a quality and cannot give a volume. By that afternoon the roll carried both, more than any finding on the Meniscus had ever carried, and the forty-one leaves went into the press with the rest.

The depot bag came up from the yards at the eighth hour with the mail off the joint tile, and in it were six lines in a small institutional hand, dated at the Dry Ferry: that Denet Rasca, sealed of the College of Levels, had surveyed the plain's centre, sealed his findings in the spring, entered nothing on the roll, and had carried the packet on his person since.

Not one thing in it was untrue.

The College sat that evening under the meridian slit, and its statutes left it nothing to decide. A seal is what the College owns. A sealed man who files nothing is a man with a lead stamp, and the remedy is to lift it. Denet Rasca put his stamp on the table before anybody asked for it, which the keeper said afterwards was the only part of the business she had any comfort in.

He worked it out later on the back of a chandler's bill, because he could not be in a room with two times and a pencil and leave them apart. The entry had gone in a little after the second hour. The bag had come up at the eighth. Four hours and some, at the end of five months and eight hundred miles.

He had won it. It was the one sequence in the whole business that went his way, and the finding stands on the roll where anybody may read it, beside a note in a second hand recording that the surveyor's seal was lifted.

To argue with a line of it a man would have to go and stand on the ground and put an instrument down. The ground is held in trust by the Wardenry until the hazard is surveyed and lifted, and the Wardenry appoints the surveyor.

## Salt and Water

Iseul Fane came onto the third step at Bell Harrow through the kiln smoke on the twenty-ninth day after the speaking of it, and went to the licence office without washing.

She entered the seal with eleven days of the forty in hand: the ground about the water at Benchmark One, declared a hazard before Oreth Vask and Sabra Onn, held until surveyed and lifted. Then, beside the Tamm Line's roll number, the cause. The halves went into a box with a hinged lid, where the halves go.

The third entry took her longer than the other two together.

"The Wardenry shall pay the finder the worth of the ground at the last assessed price of salt. The finder is Denet Rasca, Warden-surveyor, sealed of the College of Levels."

The clerk read it back, and stopped, and asked her to say it again. She said it again.

"Then we owe it to ourselves."

"Yes."

"There is no head in the book for that."

"Then make one," said Iseul Fane, "because it is in the Article, and the Article is the reason any of us are paid at all."

She entered at the foot that the requirement had been spoken and the tile broken before the speaking of it, and the hour of both. It ran to three lines, and no clerk at Bell Harrow ever read it twice.

Before she went to wash she asked when the surveyor would be appointed. She was told he was not yet appointed.

In the spring they gave her the Second Rank and a seat, which is what the Wardenry does with a Warden of the Third who gets an entry in early. She took it, and said her reason out loud, being incapable of not saying things out loud: the surveyor is appointed in a room.

She has been in the room since. The surveyor is not yet appointed; there is always a reason, and none of them dishonest. She has stopped being able to say which man round that table is holding it up, because there is not one. She still says the Wardenry.

The compensation was assessed that autumn at the last assessed price of salt, which the Assembly assesses: four blocks of first grade and a quarter, and it was paid.

Denet Rasca worked it in the margin of the paying order twice and got it right both times. Then he worked the other sum, which nobody had asked for. Four blocks of first grade, paid at a licence office window at Bell Harrow in the spring, for passage on a nine-wagon line — one man, his own water, no freight above what he carries.

He came out of the water at the centre of the Meniscus a quarter of a block to the good.

He is at Tallow Reach, stamping way-bills in the licence office three streets below the Level House, because a man may not set an instrument without a College seal. He writes to Bell Harrow once a year to ask whether the surveyor has been appointed, and gets the same sentence back every time.

Ysoly Tamm sold the Line at the outbound pound at Tallow Reach, nine wagons and forty hask at the arriving price, and came home that autumn on another master's tile, entered in another Warden's hand: one woman, her own water, no freight above what she carries.

Vask kept every term. The paper on the wagons was cancelled, the retirement sum paid in the autumn instead of owed, a season's wage entered for each of the eleven, Pell Corrow's sixty-four blocks cancelled at Bram's. Enna Corrow will not go on any paper at fourteen. Her father is out on the salt this season with a needle in his hat, talking, and Bris answers him about the leader on eight and about nothing else.

Ysoly has a room on the fourth step with a window that looks down the terraces at eight hundred miles of white. She has never looked out of it. On a shelf there is a yard of black four-sided rim-oak that is not a book of account any more, because a tally is one only while it is cut by the master of a licensed crossing. It is a piece of wood with a name on it, and the name is nowhere else: the tile is in halves in a box down the hill.

She counts nothing. There is nothing in the room to count twice.

Hanei came up before the spring crossing, because you go and see the master. Ysoly did not ask about the centre. She asked about the string — how many head, how many teams, how many spare, how many days to the Cold Well — and listened with her hands on her knees.

"Say it again."

Hanei said it again.

"Now it's said," Ysoly said.

The joint tile went out at first light with thirty-six wagons, and the tower rang it out once for each, and the quay stopped and listened, because thirty-six strokes takes a long time. Hanei counted them from the fourth wagon's box. Thirty-six.

Sabra Onn went out as water-master at sixty-two, because the water at the centre is under a hundred blocks of salt and a lead disc on a pin, and somebody must weigh what a crossing carries. She counts thirty-one crossings and will not count the one in RR 203.

On the twentieth day the wheels stopped answering and the drovers walked the hasks over the brown ground by hand, as it is done now. Everything is still there: the cairn with the bronze plug worn bright, eleven numbered stakes and their spoil rings, a mound with a splayed print beside it, an iron pin and a lead disc, and a patch of trodden ground gone brown again. Two hundred people looked at it going past, and the drivers told the first-timers what it was.

Then Sabra Onn walked a hundred yards clear of the beacon line, cut the lid off a rotten patch with a bar, and let the string drink the brine standing under it — a spade's depth down, two hundred paces from ground the Wardenry holds in trust, and nine feet above water no animal on the Meniscus can tell the difference of.

At dusk Hanei went down the string with her hand out, touching every butt at the hoop as she came to it, all the way to the last wagon. Then she turned round and came back up the line and counted them again.

Nobody said anything about it. Nobody ever did.
